The Relationship Between CBS Definition and Category Classification for Compliance with IACS UR E26 & E27

Understanding how Computer Based Systems are classified, protected, and networked under the IACS Unified Requirements

Captain Ethan
Maritime 4.0 Researcher · LinkedIn · Crew Page
If CBS is not clearly defined, critical vessel systems — such as propulsion, steering, and power management — become vulnerable to cyberattacks and operational failures, posing serious risks to safety and class certification. IACS UR E26 and E27 mandate CBS protection to mitigate these risks, and non-compliance can result in certification delays and operational disruptions. Shipowners, shipyards, equipment manufacturers, and classification societies must collaborate to establish clear CBS standards. CBS Definition in IACS UR E26

01
⚡ Why It Matters

CBS — Computer Based System — is the foundational term in IACS UR E26. Every cybersecurity requirement in E26 flows from this definition. Misunderstanding what a CBS is leads to gaps in scope, incomplete risk assessments, and failed class surveys.

📖 Official Definition — IACS UR E26, Sec.2 Definitions

"A programmable electronic device, or interoperable set of programmable electronic devices, organized to achieve one or more specified purposes such as collection, processing, maintenance, use, sharing, dissemination, or disposition of information.

CBSs onboard include IT and OT systems.

A CBS may be a combination of subsystems connected via network.

Onboard CBSs may be connected directly or via public means of communications (e.g. Internet) to ashore CBSs, other vessels' CBSs and/or other facilities."

Source: IACS UR E26 (Rev.3, 2024), Section 2 — Definitions

🔑 Three Key Aspects of the CBS Definition

  • Includes both IT and OT systems: CBS encompasses Information Technology (IT) systems (e.g., ERP, document management) and Operational Technology (OT) systems (e.g., ECDIS, propulsion control, power management) — both fall under the same cybersecurity framework.
  • Single device or multi-system network: A CBS may be a standalone programmable device or a combination of multiple subsystems interconnected via a vessel network. The scope is intentionally broad to capture modern integrated ship systems.
  • Potential connection to external networks: Onboard CBSs may connect directly or via public communications (e.g., VSAT, internet) to shore-based CBSs, other vessels, or facilities — introducing attack surfaces that must be actively managed.

🚨 Importance of a Clear CBS Definition

  • Cybersecurity scope: Without a precise definition, critical systems (propulsion, steering, power management) may be excluded from protection measures — creating life-threatening vulnerabilities.
  • Operational continuity: CBS systems must remain functional during blackout scenarios or cyberattack events; undefined systems cannot be hardened against failure.
  • Class certification & insurance: IACS UR E26 compliance is mandatory for new builds (keels laid on/after 1 Jan 2024). Non-compliance affects class certificate issuance and may void P&I cover for cyber incidents.

Key Requirements of IACS UR E26

02
⚡ Why It Matters

UR E26 translates the CBS definition into enforceable engineering requirements. Understanding these requirements is essential for shipyards designing systems, equipment makers seeking type approval, and owners managing cyber risk.

🛡️ (1) System and Network Protection

  • Cybersecurity measures must be applied to all IT and OT systems that fall within the CBS definition.
  • Network segmentation and access control are mandatory — CBS networks must be isolated from passenger, crew welfare, and untrusted IP networks.
  • Regular security updates and patch management are required; a documented patch policy must be maintained.

📋 (2) Cyber Risk Assessment and Management

  • A formal cyber risk assessment must be conducted for all CBS during design, installation, and commissioning phases.
  • A cyber incident response plan and contingency procedures must be documented, drilled, and available to the crew.
  • System inventories (hardware, software, firmware versions) must be maintained and reviewed at each survey.

⚙️ (3) Ensuring Operational Continuity

  • CBSs must be designed and configured to remain functional — or fail safely — during blackout conditions and cyberattack scenarios.
  • Critical systems (propulsion, steering, fire detection, bilge) must have documented backup or manual override mechanisms.
  • Restoration procedures after a cyber incident must be tested and incorporated into the vessel's Safety Management System (SMS).

Category Classification in IACS UR E26

03
⚡ Why It Matters

Not all CBSs carry the same risk. UR E26 introduces a four-category classification that determines the level of cybersecurity protection required. Correctly classifying every onboard system is a prerequisite for a compliant cyber risk assessment.

Category Failure Effect Typical System Functionality Security Level
Category I Failure does not pose a risk to human safety, vessel safety, or the environment. Monitoring, informational, and administrative functions (e.g., voyage reporting, crew management). Baseline — standard IT security practices
Category II Failure could eventually lead to dangerous situations for human safety, vessel safety, or the environment. Vessel alarm systems, monitoring, and control systems necessary for normal operation (e.g., engine monitoring, ballast control). Enhanced — access control + audit logging
Category III Failure could immediately result in dangerous or catastrophic situations for human safety, vessel safety, or the environment. Control functions for maintaining vessel propulsion, steering, and safety systems (e.g., Main Engine control, steering gear, power management, fire detection). Highest — network isolation + redundancy + incident response
Others Systems required by statutory regulations whose loss may trigger port-state deficiencies. Vessel navigation and internal/external communication systems (e.g., ECDIS, AIS, GMDSS, LRIT). Statutory — must meet flag-state and IMO requirements

Relationship Between CBS Definition and Category Classification

04
⚡ Why It Matters

The CBS definition sets the boundary of what must be protected; the Category Classification sets how much protection is required. Together they form the core of UR E26 compliance. Misalignment between CBS scope and category assignment is the most common gap found during class surveys.

🔗 (1) CBS and Category III & Others — Highest Stakes

  • Category III systems (propulsion, steering, power management, fire detection) are CBSs where a cybersecurity failure can cause immediate loss of life or vessel.
  • The "Others" category captures statutory navigation and communication systems (ECDIS, AIS, GMDSS) — CBSs that must remain operational to comply with SOLAS and flag-state requirements.
  • Both Category III and Others require the strictest cybersecurity controls: network isolation, physical access restrictions, redundant fallback modes, and documented recovery procedures.

🔗 (2) Cascading Risk — Category I & II Connected to CBS

⚠ Cat III Risk

Compromised propulsion or steering CBS causes immediate danger — vessel loses manoeuvrability.

⚠ Cat II Risk

Compromised alarm or monitoring CBS delays crew response — eventual escalation to critical failure.

ℹ Cat I / Cat II Interconnection

Lower-category CBSs networked with Cat III systems create lateral movement paths for attackers.

✓ Mitigation

Network segmentation between category tiers prevents a Cat I breach from reaching Cat III systems.

🛠️ (3) Practical Compliance Actions by Category

  • Category I: Inventory all administrative CBSs; apply standard OS patching and user access controls; log access events.
  • Category II: Apply enhanced access control; segment from Cat III networks; include in cyber risk assessment; document alarm system backup procedures.
  • Category III: Full network isolation from IT networks; hardware-level access restrictions; redundant manual override; include in SMS drills and incident response plan.
  • Others (Statutory): Ensure ECDIS, AIS, GMDSS, LRIT meet IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3 cyber risk management guidelines; verify at each port-state inspection.

IACS UR E27 — CBS Network Classification

05
⚡ Why It Matters

UR E27 complements E26 by specifying how onboard networks must be structured and protected based on what they carry and who uses them. It translates the CBS category classification into concrete network architecture requirements.

Network Type Description Typical Systems Key Requirement
Systems directly connected to CBS Critical control networks carrying Cat III and Others system traffic. Propulsion control, steering, power management, ECDIS, GMDSS Strict isolation; no direct internet access; hardware-enforced segmentation
Other IP-connected systems Any IP-based system that interfaces with CBS but is not itself a safety-critical control system. Condition monitoring, cargo management, planned maintenance systems Controlled interface via firewall/DMZ; access logging; regular vulnerability scanning
Passenger and visitor networks Guest-facing networks for internet access and entertainment — must never reach CBS. Passenger Wi-Fi, IPTV, guest portals Complete physical or logical separation from CBS networks; no routing path to OT
Crew administrative & welfare networks Internal networks used for crew email, social media, and administrative tasks. Crew internet access, HR systems, payroll terminals Isolated from CBS; internet traffic via dedicated VSAT channel; user awareness training

🔒 CBS Network Security Measures (UR E27)

  • Physical / logical isolation: CBS networks must be isolated from all external and untrusted networks; no direct internet path to Cat III or Others systems.
  • Passenger and crew network separation: Guest and crew welfare networks must have no routing path to CBS — enforced at both L3 (routing) and L2 (VLAN) levels.
  • Access control and patch management: Category III and Others CBSs require role-based access control, vendor remote access logging, and an approved change management process for all software/firmware updates.
  • Monitoring and anomaly detection: Network traffic on CBS segments should be monitored; anomalous behaviour (unexpected connections, port scans) must trigger the vessel's cyber incident response procedure.

🚢 Conclusion — CBS, Categories, and Compliance

IACS UR E26 and E27 form an interlocking framework. The CBS definition establishes what must be protected; the Category Classification determines the level of protection required; and UR E27's network classification specifies how those systems must be architecturally separated. Together, they represent the most comprehensive mandatory cybersecurity standard the maritime industry has ever implemented.

  • CBS (Computer Based System) = all programmable IT and OT systems onboard, including those connected to shore or other vessels.
  • Category III systems (propulsion, steering, power) and "Others" (ECDIS, GMDSS) require the highest level of cyber protection under UR E26.
  • Network segmentation per UR E27 prevents lower-risk networks (crew welfare, passenger) from becoming attack vectors into safety-critical CBS.
  • Compliance with UR E26 is mandatory for new builds with keel-laying on or after 1 January 2024 and for existing vessels at the first scheduled class renewal survey after 1 July 2024.
  • Shipyards, equipment manufacturers, owners, and class societies must align on CBS scope and category assignment from the earliest design stage to avoid costly rework at survey.
