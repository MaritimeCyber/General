Becoming a Consulting Director —
The Turning Point of Leadership

Director is not the end of a goal.
It is the moment you ask yourself what kind of leader you will become.

Captain Paul

Maritime 4.0 · Consulting Organization · Sep 2026

Last Monday, I received an email from my consulting firm announcing my promotion to Director — 상무.

I opened it and sat with it for a moment.

It brought back a memory from my years at the shipyard. I remembered the team leader I respected most being promoted to 상무. Back then, that title felt different. The organisation was larger. The culture was different. And above all, the way I looked at that position from the outside was entirely different from the way I look at it now, standing in it myself.

Now that I'm here, the feeling isn't quite what I expected.

Part of it may be the domain and the scale of a consulting firm. Part of it may be that after experiencing so much over the years, the meaning of a title has simply changed for me.

To someone, a promotion is nothing more than moving one rung up a ladder. I thought that too, when I first joined consulting.

But over the past three-plus years inside a consulting organisation, I watched people reach Director and Partner. Some moved fast. Others stayed in the same grade for a long time. Watching that process, I realise I had quietly accumulated assumptions and misreadings I wasn't fully aware of.

So right now I want to set those aside, and return to what I had been thinking about ever since I decided to make the move into consulting — the meaning of Director and Partner. This time, from the vantage point of someone who has actually arrived at that place.

⚓

1. The Career Path in Consulting Is a Map of Leadership Growth

The grade structure in consulting varies by firm, country, and organisation. In a global Big 4 firm, the Career Path broadly looks like this:

Intern → Junior / Consultant → Senior → Manager → Senior Manager → Director → Partner

If I map it loosely onto a Korean corporate hierarchy:

Intern → 사원 → 대리 → 과장 → 차장 → 부장 → 임원

Though of course the reality is rarely that clean.

For people moving from industry into consulting, it's common not to have your full experience recognised. In my own experience, anywhere from thirty to fifty per cent of prior career years may not be counted, and you effectively restart within consulting's own Career Path.

I felt that gap myself when I first joined — between what I had built before and what consulting actually expected of me.

At the Junior level, what matters most is the ability to execute a given task accurately. Finding information, analysing data, building documents, finishing what you're assigned.

At Senior, that shifts. You're expected to own a domain of work independently.

At Manager, the role changes again. You stop optimising for how well you personally perform and start optimising for how well the people around you perform. Creating 500 together with a team matters more than producing 100 alone.

As you accumulate experience leading at the team level, you reach Senior Manager. At this stage, in my observation, the ability to develop business — not just deliver projects — begins to matter in earnest. Writing proposals, persuading clients, competing against other firms, winning work.

And when you reach Director, the role changes one more time.

⚓

2. What Is Different About Director Leadership?

Here are five things I believe about Director leadership — what I understand now, and what I want to practise going forward.

2-1  From a leader who solves problems — to a leader who enables others to solve them

As a practitioner, the fastest answer is often to solve the problem yourself. I did that. When something broke, I dug in directly — coded it, analysed it, fixed it myself.

But as an organisation grows, you cannot personally resolve every problem. What matters more is giving the right person real authority and building the environment in which they can give their best.

A good leader is not someone who holds all the answers. It is someone who asks the right questions, connects the right people, and points in the right direction.

One thing I'm certain of, though: when the organisation is small, this changes. A Director in a two-person team who reaches first for Director-level privileges without rolling up their sleeves is not something I've ever fully understood. In a small organisation, the leader must be on the ground — doing the work when it needs doing, solving problems directly, sitting with the same questions the team is wrestling with.

Leadership is not about doing less as your grade rises. It is about choosing differently what you should do, depending on where the organisation stands.

2-2  From project leader — to business leader

Through Manager and Senior Manager, the success of a project is what defines you. At Director, the frame shifts.

It is no longer enough to deliver one project well. The question becomes:

"What value can we keep delivering to this client?"

Not just solving the problem in front of you — but discovering the next problem, proposing a new service, and connecting it to a new piece of business.

I believe a Director must go further still. The client a Director should be looking at cannot be confined to a single account. You need to be able to look at the entire industry and market where your expertise lives.

The line of sight must expand:

Project → Account → Business → Market

Doing a project well and building a business are entirely different capabilities. Seeing a market and actually moving it is a different dimension of leadership again.

2-3  From expertise as a personal capability — to expertise as an organisational capability

Every expert holds their own knowledge and experience. Over the years I have moved through different domains:

  • AI and Data
  • Smart Ship
  • Maritime
  • Cybersecurity

They looked separate as I moved through them. Looking back, they form a single thread.

But now, as a Director, what matters is that I stop holding that thread only for myself.

Sharing what I know inside the organisation. Growing the people around me. Creating new specialists. Converting my own experience into the organisation's Capability — that is the work that belongs to this chapter.

Perhaps this is also why I started a personal blog, and why I have been building it into a platform. Knowledge held alone and used alone ends as one person's experience. Written, shared, and connected to others, it becomes something new.

2-4  Network is not the number of people — it is the depth of trust

Something that feels more important than ever, now that I've reached Director: network. But I don't want to think of it as a headcount of names I know.

What matters, I think, is this:

"When a real problem appears — is this a relationship where we can find each other?"

Spending years in one industry, people naturally connect. Shipyards. Shipowners. Equipment manufacturers. Classification societies. IT. AI. Data. Cybersecurity. Public institutions. Individuals from entirely different worlds end up inside a single industry network.

And what matters most in that network is not the number of contacts — it is the trust built through demonstrated ability and taken responsibility.

The connections I have built in the shipbuilding and maritime world carry a different weight from networks formed through distribution channels, education programmes, or degree courses. They were forged through years of working on the same problems, taking responsibility in difficult situations, and coming through those situations together.

A network is not a collection of business cards. It is a record of trust.

2-5  The most important role of a leader is to create direction

In the end, what matters most above Director level is Direction itself.

Not giving every team member the answer — but making clearly visible the question: "Where are we going?"

And then creating conditions in which each person understands that direction, feels they belong to it, moves toward it with confidence, and grows within it.

That is why leadership, I think, is closer to Alignment than to control. Not forcing people to move — but creating the conditions in which people choose to look in the same direction.

⚓

3. Building Something Yourself Helped Me Understand Leadership

I have worked across different domains and companies, but for a long time I have been building my own territory inside the Maritime space.

Smart Ship R&D at a shipyard. Then expanding into AI and Data. And alongside that — a small space I had started just to organise my own days and thinking.

That space grew slowly.

Maritime technology and jobs. AI. Cybersecurity. IMO and IACS regulations. Written one piece at a time, it became content. As content accumulated, people found it. As I talked with those people, a network formed.

And now I am expanding that small personal blog into a platform called ShipPaulJobs.

To those who have supported it along the way, I've been extending an invitation: let's build this together, and expand it together.

What began as a private project has — through the repeated cycle of creating content, meeting people, collecting information, observing how the industry moves, and writing again — become its own domain.

This experience taught me something important:

Leadership is not born from a title. It is built through the sustained act of creating something yourself and taking responsibility for it.

The organisation assigns the grade. But the leadership — that you must build yourself.

⚓

4. Director Is Not an Ending — It Is a New Beginning

This promotion carries some weight as a milestone. But to me personally, it feels more like a new starting point — the moment the standard of leadership changes.

No longer is the goal to be the person who knows the most, solves problems the fastest, or takes on the heaviest load of work.

From here, it is:

  • Connecting people
  • Pointing direction
  • Creating opportunity
  • Growing alongside the organisation

I want to be honest about something. My energy is not what it was. And there are people offering new opportunities — with genuinely good conditions — and I am grateful for that, even as I feel myself pulled in different directions. Because I'm human.

But when I look back at the path I've walked, one thing is clear: what ultimately mattered was never a single set of conditions.

The questions that stayed were:

"What work do I want to do?"

"What do I want to leave behind?"

Those questions outlast any offer.

So I don't want to set my next goal as simply climbing to a higher grade.

Solving new industry problems using AI and Data. Connecting Maritime and Technology. Where possible, building markets and helping set standards. Leading that change.

And helping the people I work with do things larger than what I could do alone.

That is what I think of as the next chapter.

⚓

In Closing

Reaching Director means something — one goal has been reached.

But personally, I think of it as a new departure where the standard of leadership itself changes.

The aim is no longer to be the person who knows most, who resolves things fastest, who carries the most work.

Now the aim is this — and I want to keep asking these questions, going forward:

  • Not what I accomplished — but whom I helped grow.
  • Not how many projects I won — but which client problems I actually solved.
  • Not how large my title became — but what business I helped build.
  • Not what I took from the organisation — but what change I left in it.

A single promotion email, received last Monday, sent me back to these questions.

Perhaps becoming a Director is not the acquisition of a new grade. Perhaps it is the moment you sit down and ask yourself, clearly, what kind of leader you intend to be.

That question — I intend to carry it for a long time.

⚓

Key Takeaways

  • Grade is given by the organisation — leadership is built by you. The title of Director arrives in an email; the leadership behind it is built through years of creating, delivering, and taking responsibility.
  • The Director's lens must expand from Project → Account → Business → Market. Delivering one project well is no longer the measure of success.
  • Expertise must move from personal capability to organisational capability. What you know alone ends as your own experience; what you share and systemise becomes something the organisation can use.
  • Network is a record of trust, not a headcount of contacts. Relationships forged through shared problems and taken responsibility carry a different weight.
  • The most important act of leadership above Director level is creating Alignment. Not forcing movement — pointing a direction that people choose to walk toward.
