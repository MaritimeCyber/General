PART 12 · Lesson 2 Resilience Compliance · Programme Maturity

Cybersecurity Compliance vs Cyber Resilience

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can articulate the difference between cybersecurity compliance and cyber resilience, describe the limitations of compliance-focused maritime cybersecurity programmes and the gaps they leave, explain the Capability Maturity Model applied to maritime cybersecurity programme maturity, describe what a genuinely resilient maritime cybersecurity programme looks like versus a minimally compliant one, and understand why resilience-focused programmes are better investments than compliance-focused ones even from a purely cost perspective.

IACS UR E26 compliance and maritime cyber resilience are not the same thing. E26 compliance means satisfying the auditable requirements of the standard: a Cyber Risk Assessment exists, network segmentation is documented, incident response procedures are written, logging is configured. A vessel can check every E26 box and still be poorly defended — because compliance is a minimum bar, not a security guarantee, and a determined attacker does not check E26 boxes before launching an attack.

Cyber resilience is a broader concept: the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to cyber threats. A resilient maritime organisation does not just have a Cyber Risk Assessment — it actively uses it to make security decisions. It does not just have incident response procedures — it tests them in realistic exercises. It does not just have network segmentation — it monitors the boundaries continuously and hunts for signs of breach. The difference is between security as paperwork and security as practice.

This is not an argument against compliance — compliance is important, and E26 provides a valuable floor beneath which maritime cybersecurity should not fall. It is an argument for building on compliance as a foundation, not treating it as a destination. The organisations that get attacked and survive are the resilient ones; the organisations that get attacked and suffer major damage are typically compliant on paper but resilient nowhere but there.

1. The Compliance-Resilience Gap

1. The Compliance-Resilience Gap
1
What Compliance Provides

E26 compliance provides: a documented baseline of security controls, a Cyber Risk Assessment that identifies known risks, incident response procedures that specify how to respond to known incident types, network architecture documentation that maps the vessel's connectivity, and a class society verification that confirms these documents exist and these controls are implemented. These are genuine security benefits — an E26-compliant vessel is more secure than one with no security programme, and the documentation is valuable for insurance, port state control, and incident investigation purposes.

2
What Compliance Does Not Provide

E26 compliance does not provide: assurance that the controls are working correctly (a firewall configured with a rule set that has never been tested may have errors that create unintended access paths), detection of novel threats (a SIEM with only the minimum required log sources and no threat hunting capability is blind to sophisticated attacks), resilience to the unexpected (an incident response procedure written for generic scenarios may be useless for a specific incident type), or confidence that the organisation can actually respond effectively to a real incident (a procedure that has never been exercised may not work under operational pressure). Compliance is a static record of security at a point in time; resilience is a dynamic capability.

3
The Gap in Maritime Practice

In maritime practice, the compliance-resilience gap most often manifests as: (1) Compliance documentation that is accurate at class survey but outdated by the next year's operational changes. (2) Incident response procedures that specify roles and actions but have never been tested with the actual crew in a realistic scenario. (3) Network monitoring that generates alerts that are never reviewed because no one is assigned to review them. (4) A Cyber Risk Assessment that was prepared by a consultant for the class survey and has not been opened since. Each of these represents compliance at delivery and the absence of resilience in operation.

2. Capability Maturity for Maritime Cybersecurity

2. Capability Maturity for Maritime Cybersecurity
1
Level 1 — Initial

Level 1 organisations have no systematic maritime cybersecurity programme. Security measures are ad hoc — they exist where individuals have implemented them, not where a programme has defined them. There is no Cyber Risk Assessment, no network segmentation, no documented incident response. Most of the global commercial fleet was at Level 1 before 2020. E26 compliance is not achievable at Level 1.

2
Level 2 — Compliant

Level 2 organisations have implemented the E26 minimum requirements: a Cyber Risk Assessment, network segmentation, logging, access control, and incident response documentation. Security is documented but not necessarily practised. Controls are implemented but not continuously verified. The CRA is accurate at survey but drifts between surveys. Incident response procedures exist but have not been tested. Level 2 is the E26 compliance floor — achievable, documentable, and auditable. Most vessel operators completing E26 compliance programmes for the first time reach Level 2.

3
Level 3 — Managed

Level 3 organisations actively manage their maritime cybersecurity programme. They have fleet-wide monitoring through a maritime SOC or MSSP, detection rules that are regularly reviewed and updated, a patch management process that actually closes vulnerabilities within defined timescales, and incident response that has been exercised at least annually. The Cyber Risk Assessment is maintained as a living document updated by operational change management. Level 3 is where compliance becomes genuine security — the controls are not just implemented but verified and practised.

4
Level 4 — Optimising

Level 4 organisations have mature, continuous improvement programmes: they measure security metrics (mean time to patch, detection coverage, hunt frequency, training completion), use those metrics to drive programme improvement decisions, conduct red team exercises to test their defences against realistic attack scenarios, share threat intelligence with industry peers (Maritime ISAC), and integrate cybersecurity into vessel acquisition, charter, and operational decision-making at the board level. Level 4 organisations are rare in maritime but represent the direction the industry is heading as E26 becomes normalised and the regulatory floor rises.

3. The Business Case for Resilience

3. The Business Case for Resilience
1
Insurance Economics

Maritime cyber insurance is now a significant market, with most major P&I clubs and hull underwriters offering cyber endorsements. Premium pricing is increasingly maturity-differentiated: organisations that can demonstrate Level 3+ maturity — active monitoring, exercised incident response, patch management metrics — are receiving lower premiums and higher coverage limits than those at Level 2 compliance. Importantly, insurance underwriters are beginning to require evidence of resilience capabilities, not just compliance documentation, for full cyber coverage. The business case for investment in resilience above compliance is increasingly supported by the insurance economics.

2
Incident Cost Comparison

The cost comparison between compliance investment and breach response is stark. Maersk's NotPetya recovery cost USD 300 million. A Level 3 maritime cybersecurity programme for a 30-vessel fleet — active monitoring, exercised incident response, regular patching, annual exercises — costs approximately USD 2-5 million per year across the fleet. The breach avoided pays for the programme many times over. But the comparison is not only direct financial — vessel detention during incident response, charter penalty clauses for cyber-induced delays, and reputational damage in a market where cargo owners are increasingly asking about cyber maturity all create business costs that compliance-focused programmes do not prevent.

3
Regulatory Trajectory

The regulatory floor for maritime cybersecurity is rising: mandatory dedicated SOLAS cyber provisions are expected in the 2028-2032 period, PSC inspection enforcement is increasing, and flag state ISM audit cyber questions are becoming more specific. Organisations that invest in resilience above compliance now will face those rising requirements with a mature programme that needs incremental adjustment; organisations that invest only in minimum compliance will face significant remediation costs as requirements become more demanding. The economics of early resilience investment are supported by the regulatory trajectory as much as by the incident prevention case.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the distinction between compliance-focused and resilience-focused maritime organisations is apparent within the first hour. The compliance-focused shipowner brings a folder of documentation to the assessment — the CRA, the network diagram, the incident response procedures — and is confident that E26 is 'done'. The resilience-focused shipowner asks what the assessment found that the monitoring didn't catch, whether the incident response procedures would actually work in the scenarios assessed, and what the highest-priority improvements should be given their threat landscape. The documentation quality is often similar between these two organisations; the security outcomes under a real attack are very different. The compliance-focused organisation has a paper shield; the resilience-focused organisation has a practised capability. After more than a decade of maritime cybersecurity assessments, the finding is consistent: compliance is necessary but not sufficient, and the organisations that survive real maritime cyber incidents are those that invested in resilience beyond compliance.

Practical first step: Test your incident response procedures rather than reviewing them. Schedule a tabletop exercise with your CSO, SSO, and shore response team. Give them a realistic scenario: 'It is 0300 ship time. The officer on watch reports that the ECDIS is displaying an incorrect position that disagrees with the GPS and radar. The vessel is in a congested shipping lane.' Run the scenario for 60 minutes: who calls whom, what decisions are made, what communications are sent, what fallback procedures are activated. The gaps you find in 60 minutes of tabletop exercise are the gaps that will cause you the most damage in a real incident. Fix them before the incident.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 42
  • E26 compliance and maritime cyber resilience are distinct: compliance satisfies the auditable requirements of the standard; resilience is the dynamic capability to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to cyber threats.
  • The compliance-resilience gap manifests as documentation that drifts, incident response procedures that are never tested, monitoring alerts that are never reviewed, and risk assessments that are accurate at survey and obsolete in operation.
  • The Capability Maturity model for maritime cybersecurity: Level 1 (ad hoc, no programme), Level 2 (E26 compliant, documented), Level 3 (actively managed, monitored, exercised), Level 4 (continuous improvement, metrics-driven, red team tested).
  • Level 2 is the E26 compliance floor — achievable and auditable. Level 3 is where compliance becomes genuine security. Level 4 is where maritime cybersecurity provides competitive advantage.
  • Marine cyber insurance economics increasingly reward maturity above compliance: Level 3+ organisations receive lower premiums and higher coverage from underwriters who differentiate on demonstrated resilience capabilities.
  • Tabletop incident response exercises are the single most cost-effective resilience investment: 60 minutes of realistic scenario testing identifies operational gaps in procedures that document review never reveals.
