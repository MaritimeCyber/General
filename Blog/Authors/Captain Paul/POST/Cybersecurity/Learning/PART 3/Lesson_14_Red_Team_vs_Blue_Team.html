PART 3 · Lesson 1 Red Team Blue Team · Purple Team

Red Team vs Blue Team

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can explain the roles of Red and Blue Teams in a cybersecurity programme, describe the structured attack cycle used in red team engagements, list the key defensive capabilities maintained by a Blue Team, explain how Purple Team exercises bridge offensive and defensive knowledge, and understand why the adversarial mindset is critical for improving maritime vessel cyber defences.

Cybersecurity is not a single-sided discipline. On one side sit the attackers — and on the other sit the defenders. But the most mature security programmes have learned that the best defenders are those who know how to attack, and the best way to improve defences is to test them against realistic adversarial simulation. This is the domain of the Red Team and the Blue Team.

The Red Team plays the attacker. They are given permission — but not information — and attempt to breach the target organisation using real-world techniques: phishing, exploiting vulnerabilities, lateral movement, persistence. The Blue Team defends: detecting, investigating, and responding to the simulated attack. The Purple Team sits in the middle, facilitating knowledge transfer between red and blue so that every red team finding translates into a blue team improvement.

In maritime cybersecurity, the adversarial mindset is especially valuable. Vessels operate under assumptions — 'our OT network is isolated', 'attackers would not target a bulk carrier' — that rarely survive structured red team scrutiny. An attacker who boards a vessel as a port inspector, or gains remote access through a misconfigured VSAT interface, does not care about the vessel's commercial role. They follow the path of least resistance to their objective.

1. The Red Team — Thinking Like an Attacker

1. The Red Team — Thinking Like an Attacker
1
Scope and Rules of Engagement

A red team engagement begins with a rules of engagement (RoE) document that defines what is in scope, what techniques are permitted, and what constitutes a critical-stop condition. Unlike a penetration test — which is typically scoped to a specific system and vulnerability — a red team engagement simulates a full advanced persistent threat (APT) campaign from initial access through to impact.

2
Initial Access Techniques

Red teams use the full attacker toolkit: phishing and spear-phishing, exploitation of public-facing applications, credential stuffing, supply chain compromise, and physical access. The goal is to establish a foothold in the target environment using whatever technique works first — reflecting real attacker behaviour rather than the checklist approach of a vulnerability scan.

3
Lateral Movement and Persistence

Once inside, the red team pivots across the network — moving from a compromised workstation to a domain controller, from the IT network to the OT network, establishing persistence mechanisms (scheduled tasks, registry keys, implants) that survive reboots and evade casual detection. In maritime assessments, lateral movement from the crew Wi-Fi to the bridge network is a recurring finding.

4
Objectives and Impact Demonstration

Red team engagements have defined objectives — 'access the ECDIS', 'exfiltrate the vessel's voyage plan', 'demonstrate ability to manipulate cargo management data'. Reaching the objective proves the attack path is real. The report documents every step of the kill chain, enabling the blue team to identify exactly where detection or prevention could have interrupted the attack.

2. The Blue Team — Defending and Detecting

2. The Blue Team — Defending and Detecting
1
Monitoring and Detection

The Blue Team's primary capability is visibility. Without logs, alerts, and network telemetry, the team is defending blind. Core monitoring tools include SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) for aggregating and correlating logs, IDS/IPS for network-level detection, and EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) for host-level visibility. In maritime, the challenge is connecting vessel systems — often with no logging capability — to shore-based monitoring infrastructure.

2
Incident Response

When an alert fires, the Blue Team must triage, investigate, contain, and remediate. Incident response playbooks define the steps for each scenario: a malware alert on the bridge workstation triggers an isolation procedure, a port-based anomaly triggers a network traffic investigation. Speed matters: dwell time — the period between attacker access and detection — averages over 200 days in poorly monitored environments.

3
Threat Intelligence Consumption

Blue Teams use threat intelligence to understand which threat actors target their sector, which TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) are currently active, and what indicators of compromise (IoCs) to hunt for. Maritime-relevant intelligence sources include BIMCO advisories, US Coast Guard cyber notices, and the MITRE ATT&CK for ICS framework.

4
Hardening and Vulnerability Management

Beyond detection, the Blue Team drives continuous hardening: patching, access control review, unnecessary service removal, firewall rule audits. Every red team finding that becomes a hardening action reduces the attack surface for the next engagement. This cycle — attack, detect (or fail to detect), harden — is the engine of security improvement.

3. Purple Team — Bridging the Gap

3. Purple Team — Bridging the Gap
1
What is a Purple Team Exercise?

A Purple Team exercise is a collaborative, often real-time, exercise where red and blue work together rather than against each other. The red team executes an attack technique; the blue team observes whether their detection tools fire; the gap between 'technique executed' and 'alert generated' becomes an immediate tuning target. Purple teaming is faster, cheaper, and more immediately actionable than a traditional red team engagement for detection improvement.

2
MITRE ATT&CK as Common Language

Purple team exercises are typically structured around the MITRE ATT&CK framework, which provides a taxonomy of adversary techniques. The red team executes specific ATT&CK techniques; the blue team checks whether each technique generates a detection event. The output is a coverage map: which ATT&CK techniques are detected, which are logged but not alerted, and which generate no signal at all.

3
Maritime Application

In a maritime context, purple team exercises adapted to vessel environments test whether shore-based monitoring can detect techniques executed on vessel systems — GPS spoofing commands, AIS manipulation, ECDIS file modification. Because vessel systems are often proprietary and poorly documented, these exercises frequently reveal fundamental gaps in log availability and baseline visibility that must be addressed before detection capability can be built.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the red/blue distinction often collapses into a single uncomfortable question: does the vessel have any detection capability at all? In the majority of assessments, the answer is no. No SIEM. No IDS. No EDR on bridge workstations. The 'blue team' is a shore-based IT department that receives no telemetry from the vessel and would not know if the vessel's ECDIS had been accessed by an unauthorised party unless someone noticed the screen had changed. The real value of a red team concept for maritime is not the sophistication of the attack — it is the demonstration that an attack of any sophistication would go completely undetected. That finding, documented and presented to shipowners, is usually the starting point for a genuine security improvement programme.

Practical first step: Start with a tabletop red team exercise. Describe a realistic attack scenario — a phishing email to the chief officer, credential theft, remote access to the VSAT management interface — and walk through it step by step. Ask the blue team: at which point would this have been detected? The exercise itself, without any tools or technical execution, usually reveals the detection gaps that need to be closed first.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 14
  • The Red Team simulates real attacker behaviour — phishing, exploitation, lateral movement, persistence — to test defences in conditions as close to a real attack as practical.
  • The Blue Team defends through monitoring, detection, incident response, and continuous hardening — converting red team findings into security improvements.
  • Purple Team exercises bridge red and blue, testing specific ATT&CK techniques against real detection tools to produce a coverage map of what is and is not detected.
  • Maritime vessels frequently have no meaningful blue team capability — no logs, no alerts, no remote monitoring — making the first priority establishing basic visibility rather than advanced detection.
  • The adversarial mindset is the red team's most important contribution: it challenges the assumptions ('this network is isolated', 'attackers would not target us') that make organisations vulnerable.
  • IACS UR E26 requires security monitoring as part of cyber resilience — the red/blue framework provides the methodology for building and testing that monitoring capability.
