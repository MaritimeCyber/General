PART 4 · Lesson 1 SOC Monitoring · Incident Response

What Is a Security Operations Center?

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can define what a Security Operations Center (SOC) is and describe its core functions, explain the SOC analyst tier model, list the key tools in a SOC technology stack, distinguish between in-house, managed, and hybrid SOC models, and describe what a maritime SOC must do differently to monitor vessel OT environments.

An organisation that has deployed firewalls, patched its systems, trained its users, and enabled logging has done the right things. But none of those controls means anything if no one is watching. The Security Operations Center — the SOC — is where someone watches. It is the organisational capability that monitors an organisation's security posture in real time, detects threats, and coordinates the response.

A SOC is not just a room full of screens. It is a combination of people, processes, and technology that together provide continuous visibility and rapid response. The people are security analysts, threat hunters, and incident responders. The processes are detection playbooks, incident response procedures, and escalation paths. The technology is the SIEM, the EDR platform, the threat intelligence feed, and the case management system.

For maritime organisations, the SOC question is complicated by the vessel. Shore-based IT infrastructure is straightforward to monitor. A vessel operating in international waters, connected by satellite, running a mix of IT and OT systems that were never designed to be monitored — that is a different problem. The maritime SOC must bridge the IT/OT boundary and the shore/vessel boundary simultaneously.

1. SOC Functions and Structure

1. SOC Functions and Structure
1
Core Functions

A SOC performs five core functions: Monitor (continuous ingestion and analysis of security telemetry), Detect (identification of suspicious activity through rules, analytics, and threat hunting), Investigate (triage and deep-dive analysis of confirmed or suspected incidents), Respond (containment, eradication, and recovery from confirmed incidents), and Report (documentation of findings for stakeholders, regulators, and continuous improvement).

2
Analyst Tier Model

SOC analysts are typically organised in tiers. Tier 1 analysts handle alert triage — reviewing alerts generated by SIEM rules, dismissing false positives, and escalating confirmed or suspicious alerts to Tier 2. Tier 2 analysts investigate escalated alerts in depth, correlating events across multiple data sources and determining the scope and severity of an incident. Tier 3 analysts and threat hunters proactively search for threats that have not generated alerts, and develop new detection rules from their findings.

3
SOC Models — In-house, Managed, Hybrid

Large organisations build and staff their own SOC (in-house). Organisations without the budget or staffing scale use a Managed Security Service Provider (MSSP) that provides SOC-as-a-service — monitoring and response on a contracted basis. Many organisations use a hybrid model: in-house staff manage the SIEM and security architecture, while the MSSP provides 24/7 alert monitoring. For shipping companies with small security teams, the managed or hybrid model is typically the most practical path to continuous monitoring.

4
Metrics and KPIs

SOC performance is measured through KPIs: Mean Time to Detect (MTTD — how long between attacker access and detection), Mean Time to Respond (MTTR — how long between detection and containment), false positive rate (the percentage of alerts that are not genuine threats), and detection coverage (what percentage of the ATT&CK matrix generates detection alerts). Improving these metrics is the continuous work of the SOC.

2. SOC Technology Stack

2. SOC Technology Stack
1
SIEM — The Core Platform

The Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platform is the heart of the SOC. It ingests logs and telemetry from across the environment — servers, workstations, firewalls, applications — and applies correlation rules to detect patterns of suspicious activity. Modern SIEMs (Microsoft Sentinel, Splunk, IBM QRadar) also integrate threat intelligence and provide user and entity behaviour analytics (UEBA) that detect anomalous activity even without a matching rule.

2
SOAR — Automation and Orchestration

Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) platforms automate repetitive SOC tasks — enriching an alert with threat intelligence, isolating a suspicious endpoint, sending a notification to the incident response team — freeing analysts to focus on investigation and judgement. SOAR playbooks execute in seconds what would take a Tier 1 analyst minutes, dramatically reducing MTTD and MTTR.

3
EDR and NDR

Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) provides host-level visibility — process execution, file creation, registry changes, network connections — on servers and workstations. Network Detection and Response (NDR) provides network-level visibility — traffic flows, protocol anomalies, lateral movement — without requiring agents on every device. In OT environments where agent installation is impractical, NDR (passive network monitoring) is the primary detection technology.

4
Threat Intelligence Platforms

Threat Intelligence Platforms (TIPs) aggregate, normalise, and operationalise threat intelligence from multiple sources — commercial feeds, ISACs, government advisories, open-source. Intelligence is automatically applied to SOC tools: known malicious IP addresses are blocked at the firewall, known malicious file hashes trigger EDR alerts, known phishing domains are blocked at the email gateway. The Maritime ISAC (M-ISAC) provides sector-specific maritime threat intelligence.

3. The Maritime SOC Challenge

3. The Maritime SOC Challenge
1
Connectivity Constraints

A vessel at sea has limited, high-latency, expensive satellite connectivity. Sending full packet capture or comprehensive endpoint telemetry from a vessel to a shore-based SIEM is not practical. The maritime SOC must work with filtered, summarised telemetry — prioritising the highest-value log sources and accepting that visibility will be lower than in a shore-based environment.

2
OT/IT Integration

Vessel OT systems — ECDIS, AIS, GMDSS, engine management, cargo systems — were not designed to generate security-relevant logs or integrate with standard SIEM connectors. Building monitoring capability for these systems requires OT-specific network sensors, protocol-aware parsing, and operational baselines that define 'normal' behaviour for each system. This is a multidisciplinary challenge that requires both cybersecurity and maritime engineering expertise.

3
E26 Monitoring Requirements

IACS UR E26 requires that vessels have capability to detect cyber incidents and report them. This is effectively a requirement for SOC-like monitoring at the vessel level. For many shipping companies, meeting this requirement will require either deploying on-vessel monitoring sensors that report to a shore-based SOC or contracting a maritime MSSP with vessel monitoring capability.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the SOC question is one of the first and most important: who is watching your vessels? The answer is almost always 'no one in real time'. Shore-based IT teams may review vessel logs periodically — if the vessel generates logs at all — but continuous monitoring of vessel OT systems is extremely rare outside of the largest, most security-mature shipping companies. The gap between the regulatory requirement for incident detection capability (E26) and the operational reality of no monitoring is significant. The most common path forward for mid-size shipping companies is an MSSP that has built a maritime-capable monitoring service — one that understands the connectivity constraints, the OT protocol stack, and the operational context (so that an ECDIS route update at 02:00 does not trigger a false positive incident response).

Practical first step: Define your vessel monitoring scope before selecting technology. Which systems generate the highest-value security telemetry? At minimum: VSAT/communication system access logs, ship network firewall logs, bridge workstation event logs (logon, USB insertion, application execution). These three sources, centralised in even a basic SIEM, provide more detection capability than most vessels currently have.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 16
  • A SOC combines people, processes, and technology to continuously monitor, detect, investigate, and respond to security threats.
  • The analyst tier model (T1 triage → T2 investigation → T3 threat hunting) structures how human expertise is applied across different levels of alert complexity.
  • The SOC technology stack centres on the SIEM, augmented by SOAR for automation, EDR for host-level detection, NDR for network visibility, and threat intelligence platforms for context.
  • Maritime SOC capability is constrained by satellite bandwidth, OT system log availability, and the dual IT/OT environment — requiring purpose-built solutions rather than direct application of enterprise SOC models.
  • IACS UR E26 effectively mandates SOC-like monitoring capability for vessels, driving maritime organisations toward MSSP partnerships or dedicated fleet monitoring platforms.
  • MTTD and MTTR are the two most important SOC metrics — reducing the time between attacker access and detection, and between detection and containment, is the core objective of all SOC investment.
