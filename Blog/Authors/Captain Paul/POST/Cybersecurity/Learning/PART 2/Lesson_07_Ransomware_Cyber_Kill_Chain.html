Ransomware & the Cyber Kill Chain

How ransomware encrypts systems and demands payment · The Kill Chain framework · Double extortion · Maritime OT risk · Backup strategy and recovery — the attack lifecycle that now threatens vessel operations globally.

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can explain how ransomware encrypts data and extorts victims, walk through the seven stages of the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain, describe how ransomware attacks have been used against maritime operators, explain double extortion and why paying the ransom is not a recovery strategy, and outline the backup and recovery architecture that provides the most effective resilience against ransomware.

In June 2017, the NotPetya malware began propagating across global corporate networks. Within hours, A.P. Møller-Mærsk — one of the world's largest container shipping companies — had 45,000 PCs wiped, 4,000 servers destroyed, and 2,500 applications offline. Every terminal in every major port the company operated went dark. The company was forced to reinstall the entire IT infrastructure from scratch, using only one domain controller recovered from a data centre in Ghana that happened to be offline during the attack. Total losses: approximately USD 300 million. Operations across 76 ports were disrupted.

NotPetya was technically a wiper disguised as ransomware — it was designed to destroy, not to collect ransom. But it illustrated with devastating clarity what a destructive malware campaign can do to global maritime logistics. Since then, true ransomware — malware designed to encrypt files and demand payment for the decryption key — has become one of the most significant threats to maritime operators, port authorities, and shipyards worldwide.

This lesson explains how ransomware works, how the Cyber Kill Chain framework helps defenders understand the full attack lifecycle, and what the maritime sector's specific vulnerabilities are. Most importantly, it addresses the question that every organisation facing ransomware eventually asks: should we pay?

Ransomware rarely begins as ransomware. It begins as an email attachment, a phishing link, or an unpatched vulnerability — entry points covered in this series. Understanding the Kill Chain is understanding where defences can interrupt the attack before encryption begins.

1. How Ransomware Works

Ransomware is malware that encrypts files on the infected system — and often across the network — making them inaccessible to the owner. The attacker holds the decryption key and demands payment (usually in cryptocurrency) in exchange for it. The encryption used by modern ransomware is typically strong asymmetric or hybrid cryptography (RSA-2048 or AES-256 + RSA) — without the key, recovery is practically impossible.

Ransomware has evolved significantly. Early ransomware in the early 2010s was largely opportunistic — spread via mass email campaigns, targeting individual users, demanding small payments. Modern ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) operations are run like professional criminal enterprises, with dedicated developer teams, affiliate networks, negotiation specialists, and customer support for victims who want to pay.

Ransomware Technical Execution — What Happens After Infection
1
Initial Access & Execution

Ransomware reaches the system via phishing attachment, malicious link, exploit of an unpatched vulnerability, or RDP brute force. The malware executable runs and begins its operation quietly, often sleeping for minutes or days to avoid immediate detection.

2
Command & Control (C2) Connection

The malware connects to the attacker's Command & Control server to receive the encryption key material and instructions. This C2 connection is an opportunity for detection — network monitoring that identifies unusual outbound connections can catch this step.

3
Discovery & Lateral Movement

Modern ransomware scans the network for accessible shares, databases, backup systems, and domain controllers. It uses built-in Windows tools (living-off-the-land: PsExec, PowerShell, WMI) to spread to other systems. Attackers may spend days or weeks in the network before triggering encryption — finding all valuable targets and disabling backups first.

4
Data Exfiltration (Double Extortion)

Before encrypting, modern ransomware groups exfiltrate sensitive data — crew records, financial documents, contracts, technical drawings, strategic plans. This provides a second extortion lever: "pay the ransom or we publish your data online." This is double extortion, and it means ransomware is now also a data breach.

5
Shadow Copy & Backup Destruction

Before encrypting, ransomware deletes Windows Volume Shadow Copies (VSS) and any accessible backups. This is why backups on network-attached storage accessible from the infected systems are often destroyed along with primary data. Offline or immutable backups are the critical protection here.

6
Encryption

Files are encrypted using strong symmetric encryption (AES-256) with the symmetric key itself encrypted by an asymmetric public key held by the attacker (RSA-2048 or higher). The victim's files are renamed with a new extension. A ransom note is dropped in every directory.

7
Ransom Demand & Negotiation

The ransom note directs the victim to a Tor-hosted negotiation portal. Demands range from tens of thousands to tens of millions of dollars in Bitcoin or Monero. Payment does not guarantee decryption — some groups take payment and disappear; others provide a slow or partial decryptor. Law enforcement increasingly monitors ransom payments.

2. The Cyber Kill Chain — Mapping the Full Attack Lifecycle

The Cyber Kill Chain was developed by Lockheed Martin in 2011, adapted from the military concept of a kill chain (the process required to identify and engage a target). In cybersecurity, the Kill Chain describes the sequential stages a threat actor must complete to achieve their objective. The key insight is that breaking any link in the chain prevents the attack — defenders do not need to stop all seven stages, only one.

Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain — 7 Stages
# Stage Ransomware Context Defender Opportunity
1 Reconnaissance Attacker researches target org: identifies employees (LinkedIn), email format, exposed services (Shodan), unpatched systems. Reduce public exposure; monitor for scanning activity; use threat intelligence.
2 Weaponisation Attacker prepares the delivery: crafts phishing email, embeds ransomware in a document macro, or configures an exploit kit. Limited control at this stage — preparation happens off-site. Focus on next stages.
3 Delivery Phishing email delivered; malicious attachment or link sent; or exploit delivered via web drive-by. Email security (SPF/DKIM/DMARC), anti-phishing gateway, URL filtering, attachment sandboxing.
4 Exploitation User opens the malicious document; macro runs; or a software vulnerability (unpatched RDP, VPN, Log4Shell) is exploited. Patch management; disable macros; endpoint protection (EDR); least privilege.
5 Installation Ransomware dropper installs the main payload; establishes persistence via registry run keys, scheduled tasks, or services. EDR behavioural detection; application whitelisting; privileged access management.
6 C2 (Command & Control) Ransomware establishes encrypted C2 channel; receives encryption keys, updates instructions; may be maintained for days or weeks. Network detection (NDR); DNS filtering; egress monitoring; zero-trust network access.
7 Actions on Objectives Lateral movement, data exfiltration, backup destruction, then encryption. Ransom note dropped. Network segmentation (limits lateral movement); immutable backups; incident response plan.

The Kill Chain also underpins the MITRE ATT&CK framework — a more granular taxonomy of adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) that security operations centres use to map detected activity to known attack patterns and threat actor groups. ATT&CK is the practical implementation of Kill Chain thinking at operational depth.

3. Maritime-Specific Ransomware Risk

The maritime sector presents a distinctive risk profile for ransomware for several reasons that compound the standard corporate threat:

🚢 Operational Continuity Pressure

A vessel in transit or at port cannot simply "shut down and recover." Port slot windows are fixed; charter party obligations have financial penalties for delays; vessel management systems (VMS) and port management systems (PMS) are time-critical. This time pressure makes maritime operators more likely to pay the ransom quickly.

🔧 OT/IT Convergence

Modern vessels run integrated IT/OT networks. If a ransomware infection spreads from an administrative PC to the ship network, it may reach systems that control ballast, cargo, propulsion, or navigation — not because those OT systems are the target, but because of insufficient segmentation between IT and OT networks.

📡 Remote & Satellite-Connected

Vessels at sea rely on satellite communications for IT connectivity. Patch management, endpoint detection updates, and incident response all depend on bandwidth-constrained links. Remote IT support during a ransomware incident at sea is severely limited compared to a shore-based office.

🏗️ Supply Chain Exposure

Port terminals, shipyards, ship chandlers, and classification society systems all interact with vessel systems. A ransomware attack on a port operator can halt container handling even if the shipping company's own systems are clean. The 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack demonstrated this supply chain impact pattern.

4. Should Organisations Pay the Ransom?

This question has no universal answer, but the security community, law enforcement (FBI, INTERPOL, NCSC), and regulators have increasingly aligned on a firm position: paying the ransom is not a recovery strategy. Here is why:

No guarantee Approximately 20% of organisations that pay the ransom do not receive a working decryption key, or receive a key that only partially restores data. Ransomware groups are criminal enterprises with no contractual obligations.
Funding future attacks Payment funds the ransomware group to develop more sophisticated tools and attack more organisations. The maritime sector's willingness to pay makes it a preferred target.
Re-infection risk Organisations that pay are re-attacked within 12 months at a rate of approximately 80%, because the root cause of the initial infection was not remediated. Paying does not address the access vector or the lateral movement capability.
Data still published Under double extortion, some groups publish exfiltrated data regardless of whether the ransom is paid — particularly when a higher profile victim can generate publicity for the group.
Legal risk OFAC (US Treasury) has issued guidance that paying ransom to sanctioned entities — which some ransomware groups are — can constitute a sanctions violation, exposing the paying organisation to regulatory action.
✅ The Only Reliable Recovery Strategy

Maintain tested, offline (or immutable cloud) backups using the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies of data, on 2 different media types, with 1 copy offsite and offline (air-gapped or immutable object storage). Backups must be tested regularly — an untested backup is not a backup. Recovery time objectives (RTO) must be defined and practiced in tabletop exercises before an incident occurs.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

One pattern that emerges consistently during E26 CRSI assessments is the backup verification gap. Companies maintain backups — that much is usually true. But when asked "when did you last test restoring from your backup?" the honest answer is often "we haven't." Or: "we test the backup process, but not the restoration." Or: "the backup runs to a NAS share on the same network segment."

The painful reality is that a backup connected to the same network segment as the infected systems will be encrypted along with everything else. Modern ransomware groups specifically target backup systems as a priority — knowing that eliminating recovery options increases the pressure to pay. If the backup share is visible from the infected machine, it will be encrypted.

The single most impactful recommendation from a ransomware resilience perspective is this: implement at least one backup copy that is genuinely inaccessible from the primary network — either offline (tape, external drive in a safe) or immutable object storage (S3 Object Lock or equivalent) that cannot be deleted or overwritten by credentials on the primary network. Every maritime operator should be able to answer: "if every system on our corporate network was encrypted tonight, what is the maximum amount of data we would lose and how long would restoration take?" If that question cannot be answered, the backup strategy needs work.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 7
  • Ransomware encrypts files using strong cryptography (AES-256 + RSA) and demands payment for the decryption key.
  • Modern ransomware groups operate as professional criminal enterprises (RaaS), with negotiation teams and affiliate networks.
  • Double extortion — exfiltrate data before encrypting, then threaten publication — makes ransomware also a data breach.
  • The Cyber Kill Chain has seven stages; breaking any one stage prevents the attack. Defenders have opportunities at every stage.
  • Maritime ransomware risk is elevated by operational continuity pressure, IT/OT convergence, satellite link constraints, and supply chain interdependencies.
  • Paying the ransom is not a recovery strategy: no guarantee of decryption, funds future attacks, high re-infection rate.
  • Offline or immutable backups tested via regular restoration exercises are the most effective ransomware resilience measure.
