AI SOC

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can describe the AI SOC concept and how it differs from a traditional SIEM-centric SOC, explain what agentic AI is and how autonomous AI agents are applied to security operations, understand the current limitations and risks of AI-autonomous security response, and describe how maritime security operations teams can integrate AI capabilities without creating new risks.

The traditional SOC model — human analysts monitoring SIEM alerts, triaging with manual enrichment, and escalating for human decision-making — is under pressure from two directions simultaneously. The volume of alerts continues to grow as organisations deploy more tools and attackers launch more campaigns. And the talent pool of experienced security analysts is not growing fast enough to match. The AI SOC is the industry's response: using AI not just as a tool for analysts but as an autonomous agent that handles alert triage, investigation, and routine response without requiring human involvement for every action.

The concept of the AI SOC has evolved rapidly with the emergence of LLM-based agentic AI. Where previous automation (SOAR playbooks) required humans to anticipate every scenario and pre-code the response, agentic AI can reason about novel situations: 'This alert pattern is unusual; I'll check three additional data sources, compare against recent threat intelligence, and conclude that this is likely a false positive because the process behaviour is consistent with the scheduled backup job that runs at this time every week.' This reasoning capability — applying judgement rather than following a predetermined script — is qualitatively different from traditional automation.

The maritime security operations context makes the AI SOC particularly relevant. Small security teams managing large vessel fleets cannot sustain 24/7 human alert coverage without either very large headcount or very high tolerance for missed detections. An AI SOC layer that handles routine alert triage autonomously, escalates only confirmed incidents, and maintains a complete investigation record regardless of time zone or staffing level addresses the core maritime SOC staffing constraint.

1. AI SOC Architecture and Capabilities

1. AI SOC Architecture and Capabilities
1
AI Triage Agents

AI triage agents process incoming alerts autonomously, performing the enrichment and investigation steps that tier-1 analysts currently perform manually. An AI triage agent for a suspicious logon alert: retrieves the user's authentication history, queries IP reputation services, checks for concurrent logons from other locations, examines EDR telemetry for the endpoint the account last logged on from, compares the current behaviour against the user's baseline, and writes a structured investigation summary with a recommended disposition (likely false positive / requires investigation / confirmed incident). The analyst reviews the summary, not the raw alert.

2
Agentic AI for Investigation

Agentic AI systems — AI models that can autonomously select and use tools, query external systems, and iterate through multi-step investigation workflows — are being integrated into security platforms to handle Tier 2 investigation autonomously. Given a confirmed detection, an agentic AI investigator can: map the full attack timeline, identify the initial access vector, trace lateral movement across systems, identify the scope of data access, draft the incident report, and propose remediation steps — without analyst involvement until the investigation summary is complete. Products offering this capability in 2025-2026 include Microsoft Copilot for Security, CrowdStrike Charlotte AI, and SentinelOne Purple AI.

3
Human-in-the-Loop Governance

Current AI SOC deployments maintain human oversight for high-impact response actions. The AI may autonomously triage and investigate; the decision to isolate an endpoint, disable an account, or block an IP is presented to a human analyst for approval — or executed autonomously with immediate notification for review and reversal. The right level of automation autonomy depends on the action's reversibility and impact: auto-blocking a threat-intel-confirmed malicious IP is low-risk and high-value to automate; auto-disabling a senior officer's account based on an anomaly score requires human confirmation.

4
Continuous Learning

AI SOC systems improve through feedback: analyst dispositions on AI-triaged alerts (confirmed true/false positive) fine-tune the AI triage model over time. Patterns identified in human-investigated incidents are used to generate new AI detection hypotheses. The AI SOC is not a static deployment — it learns from every alert, every investigation, and every disposition, continuously improving its accuracy and reducing false positive rates as the model adapts to the specific patterns of the organisation's environment.

2. Current Limitations and Risks

2. Current Limitations and Risks
1
Hallucination and Factual Errors

LLM-based AI SOC components can generate plausible but incorrect investigation conclusions — a phenomenon known as hallucination. An AI that confidently reports a false positive disposition for a genuine incident based on an incorrect threat intelligence lookup or a flawed reasoning chain represents a meaningful operational risk in security-critical decisions. AI SOC outputs must be treated as drafts that require human verification for high-stakes decisions, not as authoritative conclusions.

2
Coverage Gaps for Maritime OT

AI SOC platforms trained on enterprise IT environments have limited awareness of maritime-specific systems, protocols, and normal behaviour patterns. An AI triage agent that correctly identifies a phishing email in the corporate email system may have no context for interpreting a suspicious NMEA sentence injection in the vessel's AIS system, or an unusual Modbus command sequence on the engine room OT network. Maritime AI SOC deployments require domain-specific training data and maritime-aware model fine-tuning.

3
Adversarial AI Evasion

Sophisticated attackers are beginning to develop techniques specifically designed to evade AI-based detection: carefully crafted attack sequences that stay within the normal behaviour baseline, API calls to AI-integrated security tools designed to trigger prompt injection, and timing-based attacks that exploit delays in AI model inference. As AI-based defences become more prevalent, adversarial techniques designed specifically to evade them will become more common.

4
Dependency and Over-Reliance

AI SOC systems can create a problematic dependency: teams that deploy AI triage may reduce analyst headcount, and then find themselves unable to effectively investigate when the AI system fails, is unavailable, or makes systematic errors. Maintaining human analyst capability alongside AI automation — ensuring that analysts continue to perform manual investigations regularly and remain proficient — is critical for operational resilience.

3. Maritime AI SOC Implementation

3. Maritime AI SOC Implementation
1
Phased AI Integration

Maritime AI SOC implementation should be phased: Phase 1 — AI-assisted enrichment (AI tools enrich alerts with context; humans make all triage decisions). Phase 2 — AI-recommended disposition (AI provides a recommended true/false positive disposition with reasoning; human analyst approves or overrides). Phase 3 — AI-autonomous triage for low-severity alerts (AI autonomously closes clearly false positives; escalates ambiguous and high-severity alerts). Phase 4 — AI-autonomous routine response (AI executes pre-approved response actions for confirmed, well-understood incident types). This phased approach builds confidence in AI accuracy before expanding autonomous action.

2
Fleet-Scale AI SOC Benefits

For a maritime operator with 50+ vessels, an AI SOC enables fleet-scale monitoring that would be operationally infeasible with human-only coverage. AI agents that monitor all 50 vessels simultaneously, correlate alerts across the fleet (detecting the same attack technique appearing on multiple vessels — a potential fleet-wide campaign), and provide autonomous triage 24/7 enable the security team to focus on investigation and response to confirmed incidents rather than drowning in manual triage across 50 independent vessel alert queues.

3
Maritime-Specific AI Context

For the AI SOC to be effective in maritime environments, it requires maritime-specific context: vessel profiles (which systems are on each vessel, what are their normal behaviour patterns, which vendors provide maintenance access), fleet network topology (which IP ranges correspond to which vessels and systems), maritime threat intelligence (sector-specific attack campaigns, maritime ISAC advisories), and operational context integration (is this vessel currently in port? are there third-party technicians on board?). Building this context layer is the primary maritime-specific engineering effort in AI SOC deployment.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments in 2026, the AI SOC conversation is increasingly relevant to maritime operators who are trying to solve the 24/7 monitoring problem without the budget for a large security team. The pitch is compelling: deploy an AI triage agent, get 24/7 alert coverage for the cost of AI compute rather than three SOC analysts. The reality check is equally important: AI SOC tools trained on enterprise IT environments have essentially no maritime domain knowledge. The first 3-6 months of any maritime AI SOC deployment will be characterised by high false positive rates on maritime-normal activity (chart update downloads triggering 'data exfiltration' alerts, AIS transponder communications triggering 'unusual outbound connection' alerts) until the AI model is tuned to the maritime environment. Investment in AI SOC tooling must be accompanied by investment in the maritime-specific training data and context that makes the tools useful.

Practical first step: Before evaluating AI SOC tools, document your maritime-specific 'known normal' activity: the IP ranges and times for chart update services, the scheduled maintenance access windows for each third-party vendor, the seasonal patterns in vessel connectivity (reduced VSAT during tropical storm season, increased bandwidth near ports). This 'known normal' documentation is the foundation for AI SOC tuning — and it will make every other security tool you deploy more effective, not just the AI layer.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 27
  • The AI SOC uses AI agents and LLMs to handle alert triage, investigation, and routine response autonomously — addressing the analyst headcount constraint that makes 24/7 vessel fleet monitoring operationally challenging.
  • Agentic AI systems can reason about novel situations and execute multi-step investigation workflows, going beyond SOAR's predetermined playbook model to handle scenarios that were not pre-coded.
  • Human-in-the-loop governance maintains human oversight for high-impact response actions while enabling autonomous handling of lower-risk, deterministic responses.
  • Current AI SOC limitations include LLM hallucination risk, coverage gaps for maritime OT environments, adversarial evasion capability, and the operational risk of analyst skill atrophy from over-reliance on automation.
  • Fleet-scale AI SOC enables simultaneous monitoring of large vessel fleets and cross-vessel campaign correlation at a cost structure that is infeasible with human-only coverage.
  • Maritime AI SOC deployment requires significant maritime-specific tuning — vessel profiles, normal behaviour baselines, maritime threat intelligence — to achieve the false positive rates necessary for operational viability.
