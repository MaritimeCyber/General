Cyber Threats Against Maritime IT/OT

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can describe the specific cyber threats most relevant to maritime vessel IT and OT systems, explain how GPS spoofing and AIS manipulation work and their navigation consequences, describe the documented maritime ransomware incidents (Maersk NotPetya, CMA CGM) and their operational impact, identify the attack paths most commonly exploited in maritime cyber incidents, and explain how the MITRE ATT&CK for ICS framework maps to specific maritime vessel attack scenarios.

Maritime cybersecurity is not theoretical. There is a documented history of cyber attacks on maritime infrastructure — some causing hundreds of millions of dollars in damage, others causing near-misses that have gone unreported due to reputational concerns. Understanding the actual threat landscape — what attackers have done, what they are capable of doing, and what the consequences have been — is the starting point for prioritising maritime cyber defences.

The threat landscape in maritime has two distinct categories. IT threats — ransomware, phishing, business email compromise — attack the vessel's administrative and communications systems using techniques identical to enterprise IT attacks. The maritime context amplifies the impact: a vessel with encrypted navigation planning software cannot plan voyages; a charter company with compromised email cannot coordinate cargo. OT threats — GPS spoofing, AIS manipulation, ECDIS chart data corruption, engine management system compromise — attack systems unique to maritime operations and have consequences that range from operational disruption to physical danger.

The boundary between IT and OT threats is increasingly blurred. The most dangerous maritime cyber attack scenarios combine both: an IT compromise (ransomware or targeted intrusion via phishing or remote access exploitation) that pivots to OT systems through the IT/OT convergence points described in Lesson 29. This combined attack path — IT initial access, OT lateral movement, OT impact — is the primary scenario addressed by IACS UR E26.

1. Maritime IT Threats — Documented Incidents

1. Maritime IT Threats — Documented Incidents
1
NotPetya/Maersk (2017)

The NotPetya attack on Maersk is the most consequential documented maritime cyber incident. NotPetya — a destructive malware disguised as ransomware — spread via the EternalBlue exploit across Maersk's global IT network in June 2017. It destroyed 45,000 PCs, 4,000 servers, and the entire active directory. Maersk could not process shipments, could not communicate with ports, and could not operate its terminal business for nearly two weeks. Total impact: approximately USD 300 million. Recovery required reinstalling 45,000 machines and rebuilding the entire network infrastructure — managed largely because a domain controller in Ghana had been powered off during a power outage and survived, providing the only copy of the AD database.

2
CMA CGM Ransomware (2020)

In September 2020, French container shipping company CMA CGM was hit by Ragnar Locker ransomware, encrypting parts of its IT infrastructure and forcing the company to take its customer booking systems offline for several days. CMA CGM was the fourth major container line hit by a significant cyber incident in three years — following Maersk (NotPetya), COSCO (ransomware), and MSC (malware). The pattern of major container lines being targeted reflects both the value of shipping company data and the financial motivation for ransomware targeting large maritime organisations.

3
Business Email Compromise in Maritime

Business email compromise (BEC) targeting maritime charter payments and cargo financing has become the highest-volume financial fraud in the maritime sector. Attackers compromise email accounts of brokers, charterers, or freight forwarders and redirect payment instructions for charter hire, freight payments, or cargo purchase invoices. Individual incidents have resulted in losses of USD 100,000 to USD 5 million. The maritime sector's reliance on email for financial communication — without electronic invoicing or multi-party payment authentication systems — makes it particularly vulnerable to BEC.

2. Maritime OT Threats — Navigation and Sensor Attacks

2. Maritime OT Threats — Navigation and Sensor Attacks
1
GPS Spoofing

GPS spoofing is the transmission of false GPS signals at higher power than the legitimate GPS satellite signals, causing a GPS receiver to display an incorrect position. In the maritime context, GPS spoofing has been used to: manipulate the positions displayed on vessels in the Black Sea (2017 incident — multiple vessels reported GPS positions showing them on land), create false traffic patterns in ports to confuse traffic management systems, and potentially redirect vessels off course without the crew's knowledge if the spoofed position is not cross-checked against other position sources (radar, AIS, visual). GPS spoofing does not require the attacker to compromise any vessel system — it only requires transmitting radio signals in the vessel's vicinity.

2
AIS Manipulation

AIS (Automatic Identification System) is the maritime collision avoidance and traffic management system, broadcasting vessel identity, position, speed, and course. AIS signals are transmitted over VHF radio and received by other vessels and maritime authorities. AIS has no authentication — any transmitter can send any AIS message with any MMSI number. AIS spoofing creates false vessels (ghost ships) in vessel tracking systems; AIS jamming prevents a vessel's position from being tracked; and direct AIS message injection can place a fake vessel in a busy fairway, potentially triggering emergency manoeuvres on real vessels. AIS manipulation has been used for sanctions evasion — tankers turning off or falsifying AIS to hide their position during illicit cargo transfers.

3
ECDIS Attacks

ECDIS systems are primary navigation instruments under SOLAS; a failure of the ECDIS requires falling back to paper charts. ECDIS attack vectors include: malicious chart updates (corrupted chart data files distributed through the chart update supply chain), direct compromise through network access (if the ECDIS is networked and reachable from the vessel IT segment), and NMEA data injection (sending false position, speed, or course data to the ECDIS sensor input, causing it to display incorrect navigation information). A corrupted ECDIS that displays inaccurate depth contours or removes hazard markings could direct a vessel into shallow water without triggering immediate alarm.

3. Attack Paths and ATT&CK for ICS Mapping

3. Attack Paths and ATT&CK for ICS Mapping
1
Most Common Maritime Attack Paths

Path 1: Phishing email to crew/officer → malware on vessel workstation → lateral movement to VSAT interface → credential theft for shore-based systems. Path 2: Compromised vendor remote access credentials → direct access to OT network maintenance interface → ECDIS or engine management system access. Path 3: Unsecured USB device from third-party technician → malware execution on bridge workstation → spread across vessel network. Path 4: Exposed VSAT management interface with default credentials → remote exploitation → gateway control → vessel network access. These four paths account for the overwhelming majority of documented and assessed maritime cyber attack scenarios.

2
ATT&CK for ICS — Maritime Mapping

MITRE ATT&CK for ICS techniques directly relevant to maritime: T1856 (Modify Program) — modifying ECDIS software or chart data; T1835 (Manipulate I/O Image) — sending false position data to navigation systems; T1831 (Manipulate Control) — overriding engine control commands; T0856 (Spoof Reporting Message) — injecting false AIS messages; T0826 (Loss of Availability) — ransomware on vessel administrative systems; T0827 (Loss of Control) — compromise of DP system causing drift. Each mapped technique connects to a specific maritime incident scenario and a corresponding set of E26/E27 controls.

3
Consequence Hierarchy

Not all maritime cyber incidents have equal consequence. At the lowest impact: administrative IT disruption (email down, booking systems unavailable) — operational inconvenience. At medium impact: cargo management system compromise, financial fraud via BEC — financial loss. At high impact: ECDIS or navigation system compromise — potential grounding or collision. At extreme impact: safety system compromise (fire suppression, emergency shutdown) — risk to life. Maritime cyber risk management should prioritise controls based on this consequence hierarchy — protecting navigation and safety systems first, administrative IT second.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the threat landscape conversation almost always surprises shipowners. The NotPetya/Maersk case is well-known, but most shipowners assume it is a one-off event at an unusually large company. The reality is that the Maritime ISAC reports dozens of significant maritime cyber incidents per year across vessel operators of all sizes, and the number reported represents only a fraction of actual incidents — most are not reported due to reputational concerns and the absence of mandatory maritime cyber incident reporting in most jurisdictions. The attacks are not sophisticated: the three most common initial access vectors in assessed maritime incidents are (1) phishing emails to officers, (2) default or compromised credentials on VSAT management interfaces, and (3) USB devices from contractors. These are not nation-state attack techniques; they are commodity threats that basic security controls would prevent.

Practical first step: Check your VSAT management interface right now. Is it accessible from the internet? What are its credentials? When were those credentials last changed? If your VSAT management interface is internet-accessible with default or rarely-changed credentials, you have an actively exploitable attack path to your vessel network that requires no hacking expertise — only a browser. Changing the credentials and restricting management interface access to a whitelist of shore office IP addresses takes 15 minutes and eliminates one of the most commonly exploited maritime attack vectors.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 30
  • Maritime IT threats (ransomware, BEC, phishing) use identical techniques to enterprise attacks but with amplified operational impact: encrypted voyage planning systems halt vessel operations; BEC in maritime targets charter payments in the millions.
  • NotPetya cost Maersk approximately USD 300 million and required rebuilding 45,000 machines — demonstrating that IT-level attacks can cause operational disruption at a scale that threatens vessel operations globally.
  • GPS spoofing manipulates position without touching vessel systems, using false radio signals to override legitimate GPS — demonstrated in the Black Sea and now a documented threat in politically sensitive maritime regions.
  • AIS has no authentication — any transmitter can create false vessels, jam legitimate broadcasts, or inject false messages — making AIS a vulnerable element of the maritime collision avoidance system.
  • The four highest-risk maritime attack paths: phishing to workstation, compromised vendor credentials, unsecured USB device, and exposed VSAT management interface with default credentials.
  • Maritime cyber risk should be prioritised by consequence: safety systems and navigation first, cargo management second, administrative IT third — not by the sophistication of the threat.
