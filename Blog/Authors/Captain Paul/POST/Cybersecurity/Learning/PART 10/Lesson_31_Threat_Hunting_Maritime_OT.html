PART 10 · Lesson 1 Maritime OT Hunting OT Telemetry · NMEA · Modbus

Threat Hunting in Maritime OT

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can construct threat hunting hypotheses specific to maritime OT environments, identify the data sources available for vessel OT hunting and their limitations, describe hunting techniques applicable to low-bandwidth vessel environments, explain what 'normal' looks like in maritime OT protocols (NMEA, Modbus) and how anomalies are identified, and understand the procedural challenges of maritime OT hunting (connectivity constraints, lack of historical baselines, proprietary systems).

Threat hunting in maritime OT is fundamentally different from threat hunting in enterprise IT or even in a traditional industrial control system environment. The data sources are different — proprietary maritime protocols, OT network traffic rather than endpoint telemetry, and system logs that were never designed with security in mind. The connectivity constraints are different — limited bandwidth means that forwarding raw packet capture to a shore-based hunt platform is not practical. And the operational context is different — the hunter must understand not just what is anomalous technically, but what is anomalous for a working vessel in a specific operational mode.

These challenges do not make maritime OT hunting impossible — they make it different. A hunter who understands that ECDIS systems communicate with chart update servers at specific intervals during port calls, that NMEA 0183 sentences have defined fields and valid ranges, and that Modbus register reads from the engine monitoring system follow predictable patterns based on operational mode can identify deviations that would be invisible to a hunter without this maritime OT context.

Maritime OT threat hunting also serves a broader programme function: every hunt that concludes without finding a threat improves the baseline understanding of normal OT behaviour, and every hunt that identifies an anomaly — even one that turns out to be benign (a misconfigured sensor, an unexpected software update) — improves the quality of OT monitoring rules and detection content. The hunt programme is the laboratory where maritime OT security knowledge is built.

1. Maritime OT Data Sources for Threat Hunting

1. Maritime OT Data Sources for Threat Hunting
1
OT Network Traffic (Passive Monitoring)

Passive network traffic capture from the vessel OT network is the richest data source for maritime OT hunting. OT-specific network monitoring tools (Claroty, Nozomi Networks, Dragos, Waterfall Security) capture traffic from OT network switches using SPAN ports and decode OT protocols — Modbus, DNP3, IEC 61850, NMEA 2000 — into structured, searchable events. These tools are designed for OT environments: they do not require agents, do not interfere with OT system operation, and understand the semantics of industrial protocols. However, deploying them on vessels requires physical network access for the SPAN port sensor and either on-vessel storage or bandwidth to forward captured traffic shore-side.

2
Voyage Data Recorder (VDR)

The Voyage Data Recorder — mandated under SOLAS — records bridge audio, AIS data, radar images, speed, heading, and other navigational data. While primarily a post-incident investigation tool, VDR data can support threat hunting: comparing recorded GPS positions against AIS-reported positions detects AIS spoofing; comparing recorded course changes against ECDIS-planned routes can reveal unauthorised route modifications. VDR data is typically available after a voyage ends — making it valuable for retroactive hunting rather than real-time detection.

3
ECDIS and Navigation System Logs

Most ECDIS systems generate event logs — user actions (route modifications, chart queries, alarm acknowledgements), software events (application starts, updates, errors), and position data. The quality and accessibility of these logs varies dramatically by manufacturer. Some ECDIS systems produce structured logs that can be exported for analysis; others write to proprietary formats accessible only through the manufacturer's own software. Building an ECDIS log collection capability often requires working with the manufacturer to identify available log data and its format — an investment that pays off in the hunting and detection capability it enables.

4
VSAT and Network Equipment Logs

VSAT routers and managed network switches log connection events, traffic statistics, and management access attempts. These logs are typically accessible via syslog forwarding to a collection point — either a vessel-based log aggregator or, if connectivity permits, directly to a shore-based SIEM. VSAT management access logs are particularly valuable for hunting: who has logged into the VSAT management interface, from which IP, at what time, and what changes did they make? This data reveals one of the highest-risk attack surfaces on the vessel.

2. Constructing Maritime OT Hunting Hypotheses

2. Constructing Maritime OT Hunting Hypotheses
1
Hypothesis: NMEA Data Injection

If an attacker has injected false NMEA sentences into the vessel's navigation data bus, the ECDIS would display positions that differ from the vessel's actual GPS-determined position. Hunt: compare GPS position data from two independent sources (primary GPS and backup GPS receiver) over the past 30 days. Identify any period where the two GPS positions diverged by more than 50 metres when the vessel was at anchor (minimal drift expected). Any divergence exceeding this threshold that is not explained by recorded maintenance activity warrants investigation as potential GPS spoofing or NMEA injection.

2
Hypothesis: Unauthorised ECDIS Route Modification

ECDIS route files are safety-critical documents; modifications should only be made by authorised officers using their individual login credentials during normal operational hours. Hunt: examine ECDIS user action logs for any route modification events that occurred: (1) during watch handover times (when both officers are briefly away from the ECDIS), (2) under a shared or administrative login rather than an individual officer login, (3) at times when no officer was scheduled to be on watch. Any route modification outside expected patterns requires correlation against voyage planning records.

3
Hypothesis: Modbus Register Anomaly in Engine OT

If an attacker has gained access to the engine OT network and is exploring or manipulating engine parameters, Modbus register reads and writes would include values outside the expected operational range or to registers that are not normally read during the current operational mode. Hunt: extract Modbus traffic from the engine OT network sensor for the past 14 days. Compare register addresses accessed and value ranges against the baseline established during normal operations. Identify any register access to addresses not in the normal access pattern, or value ranges that fall outside operational limits.

4
Hypothesis: Lateral Movement from IT to OT Segment

If the vessel firewall has been compromised or misconfigured, traffic from the IT network segment to the OT network segment may exist where it should not. Hunt: examine firewall logs for any accepted traffic from the crew or administrative network segment to the OT segment IP range. Any traffic that is not in the documented exception list (chart update connections, performance monitoring, vendor maintenance windows) is anomalous and requires investigation.

3. Operational Constraints and Practical Adaptations

3. Operational Constraints and Practical Adaptations
1
Bandwidth-Constrained Hunting

Full OT packet capture from a vessel generates volumes of data incompatible with satellite transmission. Maritime OT hunting must work with filtered, summarised data: connection metadata (flow records — source, destination, protocol, bytes, duration) rather than full packet content; protocol decode events (structured records of each OT protocol command) rather than raw packets; periodic log extracts rather than streaming telemetry. The hunter's first step in a maritime OT engagement is understanding exactly which data sources are available and in what volume — the hunt plan must be scoped to the available data, not to a theoretical ideal.

2
Baseline Establishment

Effective anomaly-based hunting requires baselines: what does normal OT network traffic look like on this vessel type in this operational mode? Baselines must be established per vessel (or vessel class), per operational mode (underway, port, anchor, dry dock), and per season. Without baselines, the hunter cannot distinguish anomalous from unusual-but-normal. For vessels with no prior OT monitoring, the first 30 days of OT sensor data should be treated as a baseline-building period rather than a hunt period — no alerts, just characterisation of normal traffic patterns.

3
Crew-Reported Anomalies as Hunt Triggers

In the absence of automated OT monitoring, vessel crew are the most important anomaly detection mechanism available. A chief officer who notices that the ECDIS route planning software behaved unexpectedly, or an engineer who observes that the engine monitoring dashboard showed an unusual reading that resolved spontaneously, provides the hunt trigger that no automated tool detected. Maritime OT hunting programmes should include a structured crew anomaly reporting mechanism — a simple form or messaging channel that captures unusual system behaviours and routes them to the shore-based security team for investigation.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments that include OT monitoring deployment, the first 30-day observation period almost always surfaces anomalies that the vessel crew and shore-based IT team were unaware of: an OT sensor communicating with an IP address outside the vendor's documented range, a Modbus polling pattern that does not match the manufacturer's specification, an ECDIS event log entry referencing a software module that the manufacturer did not include in the software documentation. Most of these anomalies are benign — misconfigured sensors, undocumented vendor update mechanisms, software quirks. But 10-15% require follow-up investigation, and occasionally, one reveals a genuine security issue that the vessel had been unaware of for months. The value of maritime OT threat hunting is not primarily in the dramatic discoveries — it is in the systematic characterisation of the OT environment that makes everything else (detection engineering, incident response, compliance assessment) more effective.

Practical first step: Start maritime OT hunting with what you have: VSAT log access and vessel firewall logs are available on most vessels without additional tooling. Hunt #1: any accepted connection from the crew or admin network to the OT network IP range in the past 90 days. Hunt #2: any connection to the VSAT management interface from an IP not in the shore office whitelist in the past 90 days. These two hunts, runnable in an afternoon with a spreadsheet, provide more maritime OT security intelligence than most shipping companies currently have.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 31
  • Maritime OT threat hunting uses passive OT network monitoring (SPAN port sensors decoding OT protocols) as the primary data source, supplemented by VDR data, navigation system logs, and network equipment logs.
  • Maritime OT hunting hypotheses focus on navigation data integrity (NMEA injection, GPS position divergence), unauthorised ECDIS route modification, Modbus anomalies in engine OT, and IT-to-OT lateral movement via firewall logs.
  • Bandwidth constraints require hunting with filtered, summarised data: connection metadata (flows) and protocol decode events rather than full packet capture, with hunt scope defined by available data volume.
  • OT baselines must be established per vessel class, per operational mode, before anomaly-based hunting can be effective — the first 30 days of OT sensor deployment should be treated as baseline characterisation, not active hunting.
  • Crew-reported anomalies are a critical hunting trigger in the absence of automated OT monitoring — structured crew reporting mechanisms route unusual system behaviours to shore-based security teams for investigation.
  • Every maritime OT hunt — whether it finds a threat or not — improves the baseline understanding of vessel OT behaviour that enables better detection engineering, incident response, and compliance assessment.
