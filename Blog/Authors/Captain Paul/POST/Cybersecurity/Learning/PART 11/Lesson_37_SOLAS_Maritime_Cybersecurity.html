SOLAS and Maritime Cybersecurity

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can explain how the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) relates to maritime cybersecurity, describe the cybersecurity provisions in the ISM Code (SOLAS Chapter IX) and how they require cyber risk to be addressed within the Safety Management System, understand how Chapter XI-2 (Special Measures to Enhance Maritime Security) and the ISPS Code interact with cybersecurity, explain the safety implications of cyber attacks on SOLAS-required equipment, and understand the circular IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2) as the primary IMO guidance linking cyber risk management to SOLAS compliance.

SOLAS — the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea — is the foundational international treaty governing ship safety. First adopted in 1914 following the Titanic disaster and most recently comprehensively revised in 1974, SOLAS establishes mandatory technical and operational standards for commercial vessels operating internationally. Nearly every piece of safety-critical equipment on a vessel — life-saving appliances, fire detection, GMDSS communications, navigation systems — is required by SOLAS and must meet SOLAS-mandated technical standards.

Cybersecurity is not a standalone chapter in SOLAS. Instead, cyber risk management has been integrated into existing SOLAS frameworks through the ISM Code (which requires cyber risk to be addressed in the Safety Management System) and through IMO guidance circulars that interpret how cyber risk management fits within SOLAS obligations. This indirect integration means that a cyber attack on a SOLAS-required system — ECDIS, GMDSS, AIS — is not just a cybersecurity incident but a potential SOLAS compliance failure, with consequences for port state control inspection and flag state regulation.

Understanding the SOLAS/cybersecurity intersection requires understanding three elements: the ISM Code requirements (how cyber risk fits into the SMS), Chapter XI-2 and ISPS Code requirements (the maritime security framework that cybersecurity overlaps with), and IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (the guidance that explicitly links cyber risk management to SOLAS compliance). Each is addressed in this lesson.

1. ISM Code and Cyber Risk Integration

1. ISM Code and Cyber Risk Integration
1
ISM Code Framework

The International Safety Management (ISM) Code — mandatory under SOLAS Chapter IX — requires every company operating SOLAS-applicable vessels to implement a Safety Management System (SMS). The SMS must cover: policies for safety and environmental protection, procedures for safe ship operation and environmental protection, defined levels of authority and reporting, procedures for preparing and responding to emergency situations, and procedures for internal audits and management reviews. The ISM Code does not mention cybersecurity by name — but its framework for identifying and responding to risks and emergencies is the natural home for cyber risk management.

2
IMO Resolution MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3

IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 — Guidelines on Maritime Cyber Risk Management — was first issued in 2017 and revised in 2021 (as Rev.2). It is the primary IMO document explicitly linking cyber risk management to SOLAS/ISM compliance. The circular states that 'an approved safety management system should take into account cyber risk management in accordance with the objectives and functional requirements of the ISM Code'. In practice, this means that flag state administrations implementing SOLAS expect that company Safety Management Systems address cyber risk. Port state control officers in major port states (Paris MOU, Tokyo MOU) are increasingly including cyber risk management questions in PSC inspection checklists.

3
Cyber Incidents as Safety Events

When a cyber attack affects a SOLAS-required safety system — GMDSS communication equipment, ECDIS, AIS, fire detection — it is not only a cybersecurity incident but a potential safety incident. The ISM Code's emergency response procedures must address scenarios where a safety-critical system is unavailable due to a cyber incident: what is the backup procedure for navigation without ECDIS, how does the crew communicate without GMDSS if the communication system is compromised, how is the vessel's position reported to coastal authorities if AIS is inoperative? These backup procedures must be in the SMS and must be practised by the crew, just as any other emergency procedure is.

2. SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and Security Overlap

2. SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and Security Overlap
1
Chapter XI-2 and ISPS Code

SOLAS Chapter XI-2 — Special Measures to Enhance Maritime Security — introduces the International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code as a mandatory framework. The ISPS Code requires: a Ship Security Assessment (SSA) identifying security threats and vulnerabilities, a Ship Security Plan (SSP) implementing measures to address identified threats, a Company Security Officer (CSO) and Ship Security Officer (SSO) responsible for security management, and continuous vessel security monitoring. The ISPS Code was developed post-9/11 to address physical security threats — terrorism, piracy, stowaways — but its security assessment and planning framework is directly applicable to cyber threats.

2
Cybersecurity in the Ship Security Plan

The Ship Security Plan (SSP) under the ISPS Code is the vessel's operational security document. While the ISPS Code focuses on physical security, IMO guidance and IACS UR E26 both reference the SSP as an appropriate location for cyber incident response procedures. E26 specifically states that cyber incident response procedures 'may be incorporated into the Ship Security Plan'. For vessels implementing both ISPS Code compliance and IACS UR E26, integrating cyber incident response into the SSP (as a cyber annex or additional chapter) avoids document duplication and ensures that both the SSO and the shipowner have a single security reference document.

3
Port State Control and Cyber Compliance

Port State Control (PSC) — the inspection regime by which port state authorities verify that foreign vessels meet international standards — is becoming an increasingly important enforcement mechanism for maritime cyber compliance. Major PSC regimes (Paris MOU covering European and North Atlantic ports, Tokyo MOU covering Asia-Pacific ports) have included cyber questions in inspection officer training since 2020. While a dedicated PSC cyber deficiency code does not yet exist in SOLAS (as of 2024), PSC officers may issue deficiencies under existing ISM Code provisions if evidence of cyber risk management absence is found — and a vessel that cannot demonstrate a functional SMS addressing cyber risk may face detention until deficiencies are rectified.

3. SOLAS-Required Systems and Cyber Risk

3. SOLAS-Required Systems and Cyber Risk
1
GMDSS and Cyber Resilience

The Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) is required by SOLAS Chapter IV for all vessels on international voyages. GMDSS provides distress alerting, urgency and safety communications, and search and rescue coordination. GMDSS equipment includes: DSC (Digital Selective Calling) VHF/MF/HF radios, EPIRB (Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon), SART (Search and Rescue Transponder), NavTEX (maritime safety information broadcasts), and Inmarsat or Iridium satellite communication for vessels outside VHF range. A cyber attack that disables GMDSS equipment — through network compromise or malware on the GMDSS workstation — removes the vessel's ability to call for help in an emergency. E26 protection requirements for communication systems directly address this SOLAS safety obligation.

2
ECDIS and Paper Chart Fallback

SOLAS requires vessels carrying ECDIS as their primary navigation system to maintain a paper chart backup capability for the vessel's voyage. This fallback requirement was introduced specifically because ECDIS failure — including potential cybersecurity-induced failure — removes the primary navigation tool. The paper chart fallback procedure must be in the SMS, crew must be trained in its use, and the charts must be maintained and corrected. This SOLAS requirement for navigation backup is the model for E26's Recover functional requirement applied to navigation systems: if ECDIS is compromised, the vessel must have a recovery path that maintains safe navigation.

3
AIS and Coastal Authority Obligations

SOLAS Chapter V requires vessels to carry operational AIS that continuously transmits vessel identity and position. Deliberate AIS deactivation or manipulation is a SOLAS violation. If a cyber attack causes AIS malfunction, the vessel master must notify the coastal authority and flag state, use all available means to maintain position reporting (radar, manual reporting to VTS), and document the incident in the logbook. The SOLAS AIS requirement creates a regulatory context for AIS spoofing and manipulation incidents that goes beyond cybersecurity: an AIS anomaly detected by a coastal authority may trigger a vessel inspection even if the root cause is cyber attack rather than deliberate regulatory evasion.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the SOLAS/cybersecurity intersection surfaces in a specific and recurring way: the vessel's ISM Code Safety Management System does not include cyber risk anywhere, and the vessel's crew have not been briefed on backup procedures for SOLAS-required systems in the event of a cyber incident. The SMS may have detailed emergency procedures for fire, flooding, and collision — but nothing for 'ECDIS is inoperative due to a cyber incident' or 'GMDSS communication system is unavailable'. The gap is not that the crew cannot navigate without ECDIS — experienced officers can navigate on paper charts. The gap is that the procedure does not exist in the SMS, so when the situation occurs, the crew must improvise rather than follow a practised procedure. Filling this gap — adding cyber incident backup procedures to the SMS emergency procedures, training the crew on them, and verifying them at drills — is one of the most operationally important E26 compliance actions and also satisfies the ISM Code requirement to address emergency situations comprehensively.

Practical first step: Review your vessel's SMS emergency procedure section. Identify the three most safety-critical systems that could be affected by a cyber incident: for most vessels, these are ECDIS, GMDSS, and the engine management system. For each system, write a one-page backup procedure: what the crew does if the system is unavailable due to a cyber incident, who is responsible, what backup equipment is used, and how long the vessel can safely operate without the affected system. Add these procedures to the SMS as cyber incident backup annexes, and include them in the next emergency drill schedule.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 37
  • SOLAS does not contain dedicated cybersecurity provisions — cyber risk management is integrated into existing SOLAS frameworks through the ISM Code (Chapter IX) and IMO guidance circulars, primarily MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2).
  • IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 explicitly requires that Safety Management Systems address cyber risk management in accordance with ISM Code objectives — making cyber risk in the SMS a flag state regulatory expectation and a PSC inspection question.
  • The Ship Security Plan (SSP) under the ISPS Code is the appropriate location for cyber incident response procedures, integrating cyber security with the physical security framework already required under SOLAS Chapter XI-2.
  • SOLAS-required safety systems (GMDSS, ECDIS, AIS) have specific cyber vulnerability profiles: GMDSS compromise removes distress communication capability, ECDIS compromise requires paper chart fallback, AIS anomalies trigger coastal authority inspection obligations.
  • The SOLAS ECDIS backup requirement (paper charts) is the model for E26's Recover requirement applied to navigation: a documented, practised fallback that maintains safe navigation when the primary system is unavailable.
  • Port State Control inspection increasingly includes cyber risk management questions under ISM Code provisions — a vessel with no SMS cyber content may face ISM Code deficiencies and detention until remediated.
