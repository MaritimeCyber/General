The MASS Code and Autonomous Ship Compliance

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can describe the IMO MASS Code regulatory framework and its four degrees of autonomy, explain how autonomous and remotely operated vessels create distinct cybersecurity challenges not addressed by E26 as written for crewed vessels, describe the Shore Control Centre (SCC) as a new attack surface category in autonomous maritime operations, understand how AI-based decision systems in autonomous vessels create cyber resilience challenges, and explain what additional cybersecurity requirements are emerging for MASS operations.

Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) — vessels that can operate with reduced, minimal, or no onboard crew — represent the frontier of maritime technology and the next major challenge for maritime cybersecurity regulation. While fully autonomous deep-sea commercial vessels remain in the future, partially autonomous vessels (with crew on board but with automated navigation, collision avoidance, or machinery management) are already operational, and unmanned vessels in sheltered waters and short-sea shipping are in commercial service in Norway, Finland, and Japan.

The cybersecurity implications of maritime autonomy are profound. For a crewed vessel, a cyber incident that disables the ECDIS results in crew falling back to paper charts; the crew is the resilience layer. For an unmanned vessel, a cyber incident that disables the navigation system and severs the remote control link results in a vessel with no human on board, no backup navigation, and no ability to respond to collision hazards. The crew resilience layer — the human fallback that E26 relies on for incident response and recovery — is absent or reduced in autonomous operations.

The IMO MASS Code — the regulatory framework for autonomous vessel operations — is under development, with a non-mandatory interim framework adopted at IMO MSC 107 (2023). Cybersecurity provisions for MASS are being developed in parallel, building on E26/E27 but requiring significant extension to address the shore control centre, the remote command link, and the AI decision systems that characterise autonomous operations. This lesson maps the current state of MASS cybersecurity requirements and the direction of regulatory development.

1. MASS Degrees of Autonomy and Regulatory Framework

1. MASS Degrees of Autonomy and Regulatory Framework
1
Four Degrees of Autonomy

IMO has defined four degrees of autonomy for MASS: Degree 1 — Ship with automated processes and decision support: seafarers are on board to operate and control shipboard systems and functions; some operations may be automated and may be remotely controlled. Degree 2 — Remotely controlled ship with seafarers on board: the ship is controlled and operated from another location; seafarers are available on board to take control and operate shipboard systems and functions. Degree 3 — Remotely controlled ship without seafarers on board: the ship is controlled and operated from another location; there are no seafarers on board. Degree 4 — Fully autonomous ship: the ship's operating system is able to make decisions and determine actions by itself.

2
IMO MASS Regulatory Scope

The IMO MASS Code (interim non-mandatory framework, 2023) addresses how existing SOLAS and other convention requirements apply to MASS operations. The core challenge is that many SOLAS requirements assume the presence of qualified seafarers — SOLAS safety management, GMDSS communication, fire and flood response, collision avoidance under COLREGS. For MASS, these requirements must be reinterpreted or replaced: who is the 'master' of a Degree 3 or 4 vessel? How is the duty of watch maintained remotely? How does the vessel communicate distress if no crew is on board to operate GMDSS? These questions have partial answers in the interim MASS Code and are being developed into mandatory requirements.

3
E26 Applicability to MASS

IACS UR E26 applies to MASS vessels classed with IACS member societies, but its requirements assume a crewed vessel context in several areas: incident response procedures require crew first responders, recovery actions may require crew intervention, and the 'master's authority' over security decisions references a master physically present on the vessel. IACS has begun developing MASS-specific requirements that extend E26 — particularly for Degree 3 and 4 vessels. Until those requirements are published, class societies assess MASS cybersecurity against E26 with bespoke additional requirements for the autonomous-specific attack surfaces.

2. New Cybersecurity Challenges for MASS

2. New Cybersecurity Challenges for MASS
1
Shore Control Centre — New Attack Surface

The Shore Control Centre (SCC) is the shore-based facility from which a MASS Degree 2/3 vessel is remotely operated. The SCC includes: human-machine interfaces (operator consoles with real-time vessel sensor data and control inputs), communication management systems (managing the vessel-SCC communication link across multiple channels — VSAT, 4G/5G, VHF data), redundant network connections (primary and backup communication paths), and vessel monitoring systems (aggregating data from multiple remotely operated vessels). The SCC is a new attack surface with no equivalent in crewed vessel cybersecurity: a successful attack on the SCC does not affect one vessel — it may simultaneously affect every vessel the SCC controls. SCC cybersecurity is a critical infrastructure security question, not just a vessel security question.

2
Remote Command Link Security

The communication link between the SCC and the MASS vessel is the most critical attack surface in autonomous maritime operations. Attacks on the command link: latency attacks (artificially increasing round-trip latency to degrade operator control, potentially causing the vessel to miss collision avoidance manoeuvres), manipulation attacks (intercepting and modifying control commands or sensor data between the vessel and SCC), denial of service (severing the command link entirely, triggering automatic fallback to a pre-programmed safe state — if the safe state is correctly implemented). The command link security requirements for MASS are being developed by IACS, but the fundamental requirements are: authentication (the vessel must verify that command messages come from the legitimate SCC), integrity (command messages must be tamper-evident), and availability (the link must have redundant channels with automatic switchover on failure).

3
AI Decision Systems and Cyber Resilience

Degree 4 autonomous vessels use AI-based decision systems for collision avoidance, route optimisation, and machinery management. These AI systems have cybersecurity vulnerabilities not present in conventional OT systems: adversarial attacks (carefully crafted sensor inputs that cause the AI decision system to classify the situation incorrectly — a shipping container shaped object positioned to make the AI classify it as open water), model poisoning (modifying the AI model's training data or weights to introduce systematic decision errors), and sensor spoofing (feeding false GPS, radar, or AIS data to the AI system to manipulate its situational awareness). Each of these attacks requires both AI system security and sensor input integrity protection — a combination that extends well beyond the scope of current E26 requirements.

3. Emerging MASS Cybersecurity Requirements

3. Emerging MASS Cybersecurity Requirements
1
IACS MASS Cybersecurity Direction

IACS is developing MASS-specific cybersecurity requirements building on E26/E27. Expected areas of focus: SCC cybersecurity requirements (physical security, network security, access control, redundancy), command link security (authentication, integrity, availability, fallback), autonomous system resilience (fail-safe behaviour in communication loss scenarios, 'safe state' definition and implementation), and AI system security (adversarial robustness testing, model integrity verification, sensor input validation). These requirements are likely to be published as a new Unified Requirement (potentially UR E28 or MASS-UR) before mandatory MASS Code adoption.

2
'Safe State' Requirement

One of the most important MASS cybersecurity concepts is the 'safe state' — the state the vessel enters when communication with the SCC is lost or when an autonomous system failure is detected. Safe state requirements: the vessel must be able to reach and maintain a safe state without SCC communication (anchoring, heaving to, or holding position in open water); the transition to safe state must be automatic and not require SCC input; the safe state must be clearly defined in the vessel's operating documentation and tested during commissioning. From a cybersecurity perspective, the safe state is the fail-safe mechanism that prevents a communication disruption or cyber attack on the command link from becoming a navigation casualty.

3
Crew Resilience Substitution

E26 relies on crew as the first responder to cyber incidents and as the human fallback for affected safety systems. For MASS Degree 3/4, this crew resilience must be substituted with technical resilience: redundant systems (backup navigation, backup communication, backup propulsion control that operate independently of the primary systems and the SCC link), automated incident detection and response (the vessel's onboard systems must detect anomalies and implement response actions without human instruction), and shore-based remote response capability (the SCC must have the ability to initiate response actions for a vessel even when the primary operator console is compromised). This shift from human resilience to technical resilience is the defining cybersecurity design challenge for maritime autonomous systems.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During discussions with newbuild MASS project teams — an increasing proportion of maritime cybersecurity consultancy as autonomous vessel programmes mature — the most consistent challenge is the absence of cybersecurity requirements in the early design phase. MASS vessels are designed by naval architects and system integrators who understand autonomy, navigation, and propulsion — but who are not cybersecurity specialists. The E26 framework, designed for crewed vessels, does not map cleanly to MASS operations, and IACS MASS-specific requirements are still under development. The result is that MASS newbuilds frequently reach detailed design phase without a cybersecurity architecture — only then discovering that the SCC-to-vessel command link has no authentication, that the AI decision system has no adversarial robustness testing plan, and that the safe state behaviour has not been analysed for cyber-induced trigger scenarios. Introducing cybersecurity into MASS design at detailed design stage is enormously more expensive than integrating it at concept design. The lesson: MASS cybersecurity requirements should be specified at the earliest design stage, even where the regulatory framework is still evolving.

Practical first step: If you are involved in a MASS project — as owner, operator, designer, or class society representative — advocate for a cybersecurity architecture review at concept design stage, before the detailed design is fixed. The review should cover at minimum: SCC attack surface definition, command link security requirements (authentication, integrity, availability), safe state definition and cyber trigger scenarios, and AI system adversarial robustness requirements. A concept-stage cybersecurity architecture review takes two to three days and avoids redesign costs that can reach millions of dollars if the same gaps are found at detailed design stage.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 40
  • IMO's four MASS degrees of autonomy (crewed automation → remote with crew → remote without crew → full autonomy) each present distinct cybersecurity challenges, with Degree 3/4 removing the crew resilience layer that E26 relies on for incident response.
  • The Shore Control Centre (SCC) is a new critical attack surface: compromising the SCC could simultaneously affect multiple vessels under remote control — making SCC cybersecurity a critical infrastructure security question.
  • Remote command link security requires authentication (vessel verifies legitimate SCC origin), integrity (tamper-evident commands), and availability (redundant channels with automatic failover) — none of which are addressed in current E26 as written for crewed vessels.
  • AI decision systems in Degree 4 vessels face cyber attacks without equivalent in conventional OT: adversarial sensor inputs, model poisoning, and systematic spoofing that manipulate the AI's situational awareness without triggering traditional intrusion detection.
  • The 'safe state' — automatic, SCC-independent, defined, and tested — is the fundamental fail-safe mechanism for MASS that substitutes for crew fallback capability in cyber incident scenarios.
  • MASS cybersecurity requirements should be specified at concept design stage — retrofitting cybersecurity architecture at detailed design is orders of magnitude more expensive and misses critical integration opportunities.
