E26 vs E27: Ship-Level vs System-Level Cyber Resilience

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can explain the key differences between E26 (ship-level) and E27 (equipment-level) requirements, identify who is responsible for each requirement (shipowner/operator vs manufacturer), explain how the two standards interact at the system integration layer, describe the compliance gap that exists between E27-approved equipment and E26-compliant integration, understand the concept of Security Level (SL-C, SL-T, SL-A) from IEC 62443 as it applies to both standards, and explain how to document E26/E27 compliance together in the Cyber Risk Assessment.

IACS UR E26 and E27 are designed as a complementary pair — E27 establishes what individual equipment must achieve, and E26 establishes what the ship as an integrated system must achieve. Together, they address maritime cybersecurity at every level: equipment design (E27), installation and configuration (both), network integration (E26), operational procedures (E26), and ongoing security management (both). Understanding how they interact is the key to building a coherent maritime cybersecurity compliance programme.

The most important distinction is the responsibility boundary: E27 obligations fall on the manufacturer; E26 obligations fall on the shipowner and operator. But this clean division becomes complex at the integration layer — when E27-approved equipment is installed on a vessel, the act of installation and integration creates new security considerations that neither E27 (which only addresses the equipment in isolation) nor E26 (which assumes E27-compliant equipment is available) fully addresses. This integration gap is where most maritime cybersecurity compliance programmes face their hardest practical challenges.

This lesson maps the E26/E27 relationship through a practical lens: where do the standards align, where do they leave gaps, and what does an integrated compliance approach look like in practice? Understanding this relationship is essential for maritime cybersecurity consultants, shipowners, and equipment manufacturers alike.

1. Division of Responsibilities

1. Division of Responsibilities
1
Manufacturer Responsibilities (E27)

Equipment cyber resilience design — building authentication, update mechanisms, logging, and fail-safe behaviour into the product. Security Zone and Data Flow documentation — providing the shipowner with the information needed to integrate the equipment securely. Type Approval assessment — undergoing class society assessment to verify E27 compliance. Vulnerability management — monitoring for vulnerabilities in deployed products and providing patches. End-of-life notification — informing customers when security support ends. The manufacturer's E27 responsibilities are about the product; once the product is installed, the manufacturer's primary ongoing obligation is vulnerability management and patch provision.

2
Shipowner/Operator Responsibilities (E26)

Cyber Risk Assessment — identifying and documenting all covered systems, their risks, and the controls implemented. Network Architecture — implementing and documenting the network segmentation separating OT from IT, with firewall-enforced access control. Access Control — implementing authentication requirements for covered systems including those installed by manufacturers, managing user accounts and credentials, controlling remote access. Incident Response — developing, documenting, and testing cyber incident response procedures. Recovery — maintaining backup and recovery procedures for all covered systems. The shipowner's E26 responsibilities are about the system as a whole — they own the compliance of the integrated vessel.

3
The Integration Boundary

The integration boundary — where manufacturer responsibility ends and shipowner responsibility begins — is the point at which equipment is installed on the vessel and connected to the vessel network. Before installation: the manufacturer must ensure the equipment meets E27. During installation: the shipowner (or the shipyard on their behalf) must implement the E27 Type Approval conditions and integrate the equipment into the E26-compliant network architecture. After installation: the shipowner is responsible for operational security (access management, log monitoring, patch management) while the manufacturer remains responsible for providing patches when vulnerabilities are discovered.

2. IEC 62443 Security Levels in E26/E27

2. IEC 62443 Security Levels in E26/E27
1
Security Level Concepts

Both E26 and E27 reference the IEC 62443 Security Level (SL) concept. IEC 62443 defines four Security Levels: SL 1 (basic protection against unintentional or coincidental violations), SL 2 (protection against intentional violation using simple means with few resources), SL 3 (protection against sophisticated attacks using moderate resources), SL 4 (protection against state-sponsored attacks using extended resources). Maritime systems are typically expected to meet SL 2 as a minimum — protection against intentional but unsophisticated attacks — with safety-critical systems (navigation, safety) potentially requiring SL 3.

2
SL-C, SL-T, and SL-A

IEC 62443 distinguishes three Security Level variants relevant to E26/E27: SL-C (Capability) — the security level a product or system can achieve if properly configured; this is what E27 Type Approval certifies. SL-T (Target) — the security level the owner has determined is required based on the risk assessment; this is what the E26 Cyber Risk Assessment defines for each covered system. SL-A (Achieved) — the actual security level achieved after installation and configuration; this is what the class society verifies during E26 survey. Compliance requires that SL-A meets or exceeds SL-T, and that the selected equipment's SL-C meets or exceeds SL-T.

3
Integration Gap — SL-C Does Not Guarantee SL-A

The key integration insight: E27 certifies SL-C — what the equipment can achieve. E26 requires SL-A — what the integrated vessel achieves. SL-C ≥ SL-T is necessary but not sufficient for compliance; SL-A ≥ SL-T requires correct installation, configuration, and integration. An ECDIS with E27 Type Approval certifying SL 2 capability, installed on a vessel without implementing the required configuration conditions (e.g. default credentials unchanged, logging not enabled, network connected to the crew Wi-Fi segment), does not achieve SL 2 in practice — its SL-A is lower than its SL-C because the integration failed. This gap is the primary finding in E26 compliance assessments.

3. Integrated Compliance Documentation

3. Integrated Compliance Documentation
1
CRA Integration of E27 Data

The Cyber Risk Assessment required by E26 should incorporate E27 Type Approval data for each covered equipment item: the Type Approval certificate reference, the SL-C certified, the configuration conditions required for that SL-C, and confirmation that those conditions have been implemented (SL-A assessment). This integration of E27 data into the E26 CRA provides the class surveyor with a complete picture: what equipment is installed, what it is certified to achieve, what is required to achieve it, and whether those requirements are implemented.

2
Gap Analysis — E27 Non-Compliant Equipment

For existing vessels with equipment that predates E27 (or for which no E27 Type Approval exists), the E26 Cyber Risk Assessment must document the gap: the equipment, the E27 requirements it does not meet, the compensating controls implemented to reduce the risk, and the residual risk accepted. Class societies accept compensating controls for equipment that cannot be E27-certified — but the compensating controls must be documented, implemented, and verified. Common compensating controls: network isolation of non-E27-compliant equipment, enhanced monitoring, more frequent security assessment, and commitment to upgrade when E27-compliant replacement equipment is available.

3
Ongoing Compliance — Patch Management Integration

The ongoing compliance challenge is integrating the manufacturer's E27 vulnerability management obligations with the shipowner's E26 patch management responsibilities. E27 requires manufacturers to provide patches; E26 requires shipowners to apply them. In practice: the manufacturer notifies of a patch (E27 obligation), the shipowner receives the notification and assesses the patch against the Cyber Risk Assessment (E26 obligation), schedules the patch for the next maintenance opportunity, applies and tests it, and updates the CRA to reflect the new software version and any changed risk profile. This integrated patch management workflow is what the E26 Software Integrity protection requirement envisions — but it requires operational procedures connecting the manufacturer's notification system to the shipowner's maintenance planning.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the E26/E27 integration gap manifests most clearly in access control. The class surveyor's checklist asks: are default credentials changed on all covered systems? The answer is almost universally no for legacy equipment. Default credentials persist because (a) the installer did not know to change them, (b) the manufacturer's documentation did not clearly require it, or (c) the password is not documented anywhere and changing it risked locking out the only person who knows how to access the system. The practical fix requires a combination of manufacturer documentation (E27 obligation: provide clear credential management guidance), shipowner procedure (E26 obligation: implement credential management as part of the installation checklist), and inventory (knowing what credentials exist for every system). This intersection — manufacturer providing what the shipowner needs to implement the control — is the E26/E27 integration challenge in its most practical form.

Practical first step: For each covered system on your vessel, create a three-column compliance card: (1) E27 Type Approval status and SL-C certified; (2) E27 conditions required for that SL-C; (3) confirmation that each condition is implemented (SL-A assessment). This simple card format, repeated for every covered system, is the integration documentation that connects E27 to E26 in the Cyber Risk Assessment and gives the class surveyor exactly what they need to verify E26 compliance. It also identifies the integration gaps — systems where SL-C is documented but the conditions have not been implemented — that must be remediated before the survey.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 36
  • E26 is a ship-level standard with shipowner/operator obligations; E27 is an equipment-level standard with manufacturer obligations — they complement each other across the ship system lifecycle.
  • The integration boundary separates manufacturer responsibility (E27, pre-installation) from shipowner responsibility (E26, post-installation) — but correct integration is jointly dependent on both parties fulfilling their obligations.
  • IEC 62443 Security Levels: SL-C (capability, what E27 certifies), SL-T (target, what the E26 CRA requires), SL-A (achieved, what the class society verifies) — compliance requires SL-A ≥ SL-T, which requires correct integration of E27-capable equipment.
  • The integration gap: E27 Type Approval certifying SL 2 capability does not guarantee SL 2 achievement — incorrect installation, unchanged default credentials, or disabled logging reduces SL-A below SL-C despite the Type Approval.
  • The E26 Cyber Risk Assessment should integrate E27 Type Approval data: certificate reference, SL-C certified, conditions required, and SL-A assessment confirming conditions are implemented.
  • For E27 non-compliant legacy equipment, compensating controls (network isolation, enhanced monitoring, upgrade commitment) must be documented in the CRA — class societies accept compensating controls with proper documentation.
