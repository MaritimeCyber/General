IACS UR E26: Cyber Resilience of Ships

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can explain the scope and structure of IACS UR E26, describe the five functional requirements it imposes on ships, explain what systems are covered under 'computer-based systems used for ship functions', understand the verification and survey obligations for existing and newbuild vessels, explain the role of the Cyber Risk Assessment (CRA) in E26 compliance, and understand how E26 relates to the ISM Code and the Ship Security Plan.

IACS UR E26 — Cyber Resilience of Ships — is the definitive maritime cybersecurity standard for ship-level compliance. Published by the International Association of Classification Societies (IACS) and mandatory for all vessels classed with any of the thirteen IACS member classification societies from 1 July 2024, E26 establishes the functional requirements that a vessel must satisfy to be considered cyber resilient. For the maritime industry, E26 represents the transition from voluntary cybersecurity guidance to mandatory classification requirements backed by survey and audit obligations.

E26 is a ship-level standard: it addresses the vessel as a whole, the systems installed on it, the network architecture connecting those systems, the procedures governing their secure operation, and the people responsible for implementing those procedures. It complements IACS UR E27 — Cyber Resilience of On-board Systems and Equipment — which addresses cyber resilience at the individual equipment and system level before installation on the ship. E26 assumes that equipment meeting E27 requirements is available; it then addresses what the shipowner and operator must do to ensure the integrated vessel is cyber resilient.

Understanding E26 in detail is the prerequisite for every other element of ship-level maritime cybersecurity compliance: the Cyber Risk Assessment, the Network Architecture documentation, the Ship Security Plan cyber annex, and the class society survey process all flow from the E26 functional requirements. This lesson maps those requirements systematically.

1. Scope and Applicability

1. Scope and Applicability
1
Vessels in Scope

E26 applies to newbuild vessels with keel-laying date on or after 1 July 2024 and to existing vessels undergoing major conversions that affect computer-based systems used for ship functions. For existing vessels not undergoing major conversion, IACS member class societies have a transitional survey programme — the exact timeline varies by class society, but most are implementing E26 verification for existing vessels at the next scheduled special survey (every 5 years). Vessel types covered include: cargo ships, tankers, passenger ships, offshore vessels, high-speed craft, and other commercial vessels classed with an IACS member society.

2
'Computer-Based Systems Used for Ship Functions'

The key scoping concept in E26 is 'computer-based systems used for ship functions'. This includes: navigation systems (ECDIS, GPS, AIS, radar), propulsion and machinery control, cargo management systems, ballast water management, safety systems (fire detection, flooding alarm, emergency shutdown), stability and loading calculators, communication systems (GMDSS, VSAT), and any other computer-based system whose compromise could affect ship safety, security, or environmental compliance. It explicitly excludes: crew personal devices, commercial hotel services (passenger ship entertainment), and systems with no connection to ship functions.

3
Relationship to ISM Code

E26 requirements are designed to be integrated with the ISM Code (International Safety Management Code) Safety Management System (SMS). The ISM Code already requires procedures for identifying and responding to emergency situations; E26 adds cyber resilience requirements to the SMS context. Specifically, E26 requires that cyber risk be incorporated into the ship's Safety Management System — the vessel's Cyber Risk Assessment becomes a document maintained under the SMS, and cyber incident response procedures become part of the SMS emergency response framework.

2. The Five Functional Requirements

2. The Five Functional Requirements
1
Identify

The vessel must have documented identification of all computer-based systems used for ship functions, the networks connecting them, and the external connections to those systems. This includes: an asset inventory of all covered systems, a network diagram showing system interconnections and external connections, and identification of cyber risks associated with those systems. The Cyber Risk Assessment (CRA) is the primary document through which the Identify requirement is met — it maps assets, connections, and risks in a format auditable by the class surveyor.

2
Protect

The vessel must implement security controls appropriate to the identified risks. E26 specifies protection controls in several categories: Access Control (authentication requirements for covered systems, management of remote access, physical access controls for server rooms and bridge), Network Segmentation (separation of OT systems from IT systems and external networks as described in the network architecture), Software Integrity (patch management procedures for covered systems, change management controls), and Supply Chain Security (procedures for evaluating and controlling software and hardware from vendors and service providers).

3
Detect

The vessel must have capability to detect cyber incidents affecting computer-based systems used for ship functions. E26 specifies that detection capability must cover: security monitoring of covered systems, anomaly detection for network traffic between IT and OT segments, and logging of security-relevant events with log retention sufficient for post-incident analysis. The E26 detection requirement is the basis for the maritime SOC monitoring capability described in Lesson 33.

4
Respond

The vessel must have documented procedures for responding to cyber incidents. E26 requires: a cyber incident response plan integrated with the SMS, defined roles and responsibilities for crew and shore-based response teams, procedures for initial containment actions crew can take without remote support, escalation procedures for notifying the shipowner and relevant authorities, and communication procedures for maintaining vessel operations during a cyber incident (manual/backup fallbacks for affected safety-critical systems).

5
Recover

The vessel must have documented procedures for recovering affected systems after a cyber incident. E26 requires: backup and recovery procedures for covered systems (including backup media management and tested restoration procedures), procedures for restoring from clean backups where systems have been compromised, validation procedures for confirming system integrity before returning systems to operational status, and procedures for reviewing the incident to prevent recurrence. Recovery also includes maintaining the vessel's Safety Management System documentation through a cyber incident.

3. Cyber Risk Assessment and Survey

3. Cyber Risk Assessment and Survey
1
Cyber Risk Assessment (CRA)

The Cyber Risk Assessment is the central compliance document under E26. It must: identify all computer-based systems in scope, identify the cyber risks to each system (threat scenarios, vulnerability assessment, likelihood and impact analysis), document the controls implemented for each identified risk, and provide a residual risk assessment. The CRA is not a one-time document — E26 requires it to be reviewed and updated when significant changes are made to covered systems and at each periodic survey. Class societies provide CRA templates, and commercial maritime cybersecurity consultants prepare CRAs as a primary service offering for shipowners.

2
Network Architecture Documentation

E26 requires documented network architecture showing all connections between covered systems, between IT and OT segments, and between vessel systems and external networks. This documentation must include: network diagrams with IP ranges and VLAN assignments, firewall rule sets (or equivalent access control documentation), remote access configurations, and justification for any IT/OT connections in terms of operational necessity and risk mitigation. The network architecture documentation is reviewed by the class surveyor during the E26 verification survey.

3
Class Society Survey Process

E26 compliance is verified through the class society survey process. For newbuild vessels, E26 verification is conducted at initial classification survey. For existing vessels, most class societies implement E26 verification at the next scheduled special survey or a dedicated E26 survey. Survey activities include: review of the Cyber Risk Assessment and network architecture documentation, verification that protection controls are implemented (access control checks, firewall rule review), and review of incident response and recovery procedures. Deficiencies result in conditions of class that must be rectified before the next survey.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, the most common finding is that the vessel has no Cyber Risk Assessment document at all. When asked where their CRA is, the ship manager responds either 'we don't have one' or produces a generic IT risk assessment covering shore-based systems that was never adapted for the vessel. The second most common finding is that the network architecture documentation either does not exist or was created years ago and no longer reflects the actual network — new systems have been added, connections have been modified, and the diagram has not been updated. Both of these are critical findings under E26: without a CRA and accurate network architecture documentation, the class surveyor cannot verify the Identify requirement, and the entire E26 compliance structure falls. The practical implication is that the first step in any E26 compliance programme is not a security tool deployment — it is a documentation exercise: build the CRA and update the network architecture. Everything else follows from that foundation.

Practical first step: Start your E26 compliance programme with a vessel walk-through. Take a network engineer and a maritime cybersecurity consultant onto the vessel together. Walk every space — bridge, engine control room, cargo control room, server room — and identify every computer and networked device. Photograph it, note its function, note its network connection. This physical walk-through, which takes half a day on a typical cargo vessel, provides the asset inventory that feeds every other E26 compliance document. Class society CRA templates are designed to accept exactly this input.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 34
  • IACS UR E26 is mandatory for all vessels classed with IACS member class societies from 1 July 2024, covering computer-based systems used for ship functions including navigation, propulsion, cargo, safety, and communications systems.
  • The five E26 functional requirements are Identify (asset and risk documentation), Protect (access control, segmentation, patch management), Detect (security monitoring and logging), Respond (incident response procedures), and Recover (backup, restoration, and post-incident review).
  • The Cyber Risk Assessment (CRA) is the central E26 compliance document: it maps assets, threats, vulnerabilities, controls, and residual risks in a format auditable by the class surveyor.
  • Network architecture documentation — network diagrams, firewall rule sets, remote access configurations — is a mandatory E26 deliverable reviewed during the class society verification survey.
  • E26 is designed to integrate with the ISM Code Safety Management System: the CRA becomes an SMS document, and cyber incident response procedures become part of the SMS emergency response framework.
  • The most common E26 compliance gaps: absence of a vessel-specific CRA, inaccurate or outdated network architecture documentation, and no documented cyber incident response procedures integrated with the SMS.
