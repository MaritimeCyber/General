PART 11 · Lesson 6 IMO MSC Cyber Guidelines · Resolution · Circular

IMO MSC and Maritime Cyber Risk Guidelines

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can explain the International Maritime Organization's (IMO) role in maritime cybersecurity regulation, describe IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2) and its five functional elements of cyber risk management, explain IMO MSC Resolution MSC.428(98) and why it is the key resolution linking cyber risk management to SOLAS compliance, understand how IMO guidance (non-binding circulars) relates to mandatory SOLAS requirements and IACS classification rules, describe the IMO's cyber risk management requirements timeline and what has been achieved, and understand the limitations of IMO guidance relative to IACS mandatory requirements.

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) is the United Nations agency responsible for the safety and security of international shipping and the prevention of pollution from ships. IMO develops the international conventions and guidelines that form the foundation of maritime regulation — including SOLAS, MARPOL, the STCW Convention, and the ISPS Code. In maritime cybersecurity, IMO's role is primarily one of guidance and standard-setting rather than enforcement — enforcement of maritime regulations is the responsibility of flag states and port states.

IMO's maritime cybersecurity framework consists of two key documents: MSC Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted in June 2017, which encouraged shipping companies to address cyber risk in their Safety Management Systems by 1 January 2021; and MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2), the Guidelines on Maritime Cyber Risk Management, which provides the detailed framework for how cyber risk management should be structured. Together, these documents established the first international consensus on maritime cyber risk management and created the expectation — now backed by class society mandatory requirements through IACS UR E26/E27 — that all vessels operating internationally have a documented cyber risk management programme.

Understanding the difference between IMO guidance (non-binding recommendations and circulars, influential but not mandatory) and IACS mandatory requirements (classification rules that are conditions of class) is essential for maritime professionals. IMO set the direction; IACS created the mandatory implementation mechanism. Both are part of the regulatory landscape that maritime cybersecurity professionals must navigate.

1. IMO MSC Resolution MSC.428(98)

1. IMO MSC Resolution MSC.428(98)
1
The Resolution's Core Statement

MSC Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted at the 98th session of the IMO Maritime Safety Committee in June 2017, contains the foundational statement linking cyber risk management to SOLAS compliance: 'an approved safety management system should take into account cyber risk management in accordance with the objectives and functional requirements of the ISM Code'. The resolution set 1 January 2021 as the date by which shipping companies should have addressed cyber risk in their Safety Management Systems. This date has passed — IMO's expectation is that all vessels with ISM-Code-compliant Safety Management Systems are now addressing cyber risk.

2
Enforcement through Flag States

MSC.428(98) is a resolution — it 'invites' and 'encourages' rather than mandates. The mandatory force comes from its integration with the ISM Code: since ISM Code compliance is required under SOLAS Chapter IX, and MSC.428(98) interprets ISM Code requirements to include cyber risk management, flag state administrations implementing the ISM Code are expected to verify cyber risk management during ISM Code Document of Compliance audits. In practice, the level of flag state enforcement varies significantly — major maritime nations (Marshall Islands, Panama, Liberia, Bahamas) have issued guidance requiring cyber risk in SMS documents; some smaller flag states have not yet integrated cyber into their ISM audit processes.

3
Port State Control Implementation

Port state control authorities have used MSC.428(98) as the basis for including cyber questions in PSC inspection checklists. Under the Paris MOU and Tokyo MOU regimes, PSC officers may ask: Does the vessel have a Cyber Risk Assessment in its SMS? Does the vessel have documented cyber incident response procedures? Has the crew received cyber awareness training? A vessel that cannot produce these documents may receive deficiency notices under ISM Code provisions. The Tokyo MOU has been particularly active in maritime cyber PSC enforcement, with dedicated cyber training for PSC officers since 2021.

2. MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2) — Five Functional Elements

2. MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2) — Five Functional Elements
1
Framework Structure

IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2) — Guidelines on Maritime Cyber Risk Management — provides the framework for maritime cyber risk management, organised around five functional elements derived from the NIST Cybersecurity Framework: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. These are the same five elements used in IACS UR E26 — demonstrating that E26 was deliberately designed to be compatible with the IMO framework, enabling shipping companies to use a single integrated approach that satisfies both IMO guidelines and IACS mandatory requirements.

2
IMO vs IACS Detail Level

The key difference between IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 and IACS UR E26 is the level of specificity. IMO Circ.3 provides high-level guidance: organisations should 'identify' assets, should 'protect' them with appropriate controls, should 'detect' incidents. IACS UR E26 provides mandatory, auditable requirements: the vessel must have a documented Cyber Risk Assessment, must implement network segmentation with firewall-enforced access control, must have logging capability with defined minimum event types. The IMO framework sets the structure; IACS provides the mandatory technical detail that makes the structure verifiable.

3
Cyber Risk Management Stakeholders

IMO Circ.3 identifies three stakeholder categories with distinct roles in maritime cyber risk management: Flag State Administrations (regulate through ISM Code, provide guidance on implementation expectations), Class Societies (implement mandatory technical requirements through classification rules — IACS UR E26/E27), and Industry Organisations (develop sector-specific guidance — BIMCO, IMCA, INTERTANKO guidelines complement IMO and IACS frameworks). The shipping company sits at the intersection of all three stakeholder categories: it must satisfy flag state ISM requirements, class society classification requirements, and industry standard expectations, using a single integrated programme.

4
Cyber Risk Management Process

IMO Circ.3 describes the cyber risk management process as iterative: (1) Identify the company's cyber risk management approach, scope, and context. (2) Identify the assets, systems, data, and capabilities that support ship functions and the risks to them. (3) Implement protective measures appropriate to the risks. (4) Establish detection mechanisms for cyber incidents. (5) Establish response procedures for cyber incidents. (6) Establish recovery procedures to restore affected systems and capabilities. (7) Review the programme periodically and after incidents. This iterative process maps directly to the E26 Cyber Risk Assessment update requirements and the SMS periodic review requirements under the ISM Code.

3. IMO Future Regulation and Limitations

3. IMO Future Regulation and Limitations
1
IMO Regulatory Pipeline

IMO is actively developing more specific cyber regulation. Key items under development (as of 2024-2026): dedicated SOLAS chapter for maritime cybersecurity (moving from ISM Code integration to standalone SOLAS requirements); mandatory cyber incident reporting requirements (creating a structured international maritime cyber incident database); and cyber security standards for ship design (integrating cyber into the SOLAS construction and equipment requirements at the design stage rather than treating it as an operational overlay). The IMO work programme suggests mandatory dedicated SOLAS cyber provisions may be adopted in the 2028-2032 period.

2
IMO Guidance vs Class Society Requirements

A critical limitation of IMO guidance: circulars and assembly resolutions are not self-executing mandatory requirements. They require flag state implementation (through national legislation or ISM Code interpretation) or class society implementation (through classification rules) to have mandatory force. IACS UR E26/E27 has filled this gap more effectively than IMO regulation for classed vessels — by the time dedicated SOLAS cyber provisions are adopted, IACS mandatory requirements will have been in force for 5+ years. For unclassed vessels (small vessels, inland waterway vessels, some fishing vessels), IMO flag state implementation may be the primary regulatory mechanism.

3
Non-IACS Maritime Vessels

Approximately 20% of commercial vessels are not classed with an IACS member society — they operate under national class societies or without class. For these vessels, IACS UR E26/E27 does not apply, and the regulatory framework is flag state implementation of IMO guidance. Cybersecurity maturity in this segment is typically lower than in the IACS-classed fleet, and incidents in this segment are less likely to be reported or investigated. As IMO develops mandatory SOLAS cyber provisions, closing this coverage gap for non-IACS-classed vessels will be an important regulatory objective.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During E26 CRSI assessments, shipping companies frequently conflate IMO guidance with IACS mandatory requirements — and in both directions. Some believe that satisfying IACS UR E26 automatically satisfies all IMO requirements (it does for the five-element framework, but ISM Code integration and SMS documentation requires additional steps beyond the E26 technical controls). Others believe that including a paragraph about cyber risk in their SMS satisfies IACS UR E26 (it does not — E26 requires specific technical controls, documented CRA, and auditable network architecture). The practical answer is that the frameworks are complementary and should be implemented together: the E26 Cyber Risk Assessment satisfies the IMO 'Identify' element; the E26 network segmentation and access control requirements satisfy the 'Protect' element; and so on. The company should maintain a single compliance matrix showing how each E26 requirement maps to the corresponding IMO Circ.3 element and ISM Code section — this matrix is the most efficient way to demonstrate integrated compliance to class surveyors, flag state auditors, and PSC officers simultaneously.

Practical first step: Build a one-page compliance alignment table with three columns: IMO Circ.3 Element, E26 Requirement, and SMS Section. Map each of the five IMO functional elements to the corresponding E26 requirements and the section of your Safety Management System where those requirements are addressed. This table takes an hour to build and eliminates the question 'does this satisfy IMO and IACS?' for every element of your maritime cyber risk management programme. Share it with your CSO, your class society surveyor, and your flag state auditor as evidence of an integrated compliance approach.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 39
  • IMO MSC Resolution MSC.428(98) (2017) established that Safety Management Systems must address cyber risk under the ISM Code, with 1 January 2021 as the implementation target — creating the international expectation of cyber risk management in all ISM-compliant vessels.
  • IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 (Rev.2) provides the five-element maritime cyber risk management framework (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) — identical to the IACS UR E26 structure, enabling integrated compliance.
  • IMO guidance (circulars, resolutions) is not self-executing — it requires flag state or class society implementation. IACS UR E26/E27 provides mandatory technical requirements that fill this gap for the approximately 80% of commercial vessels classed with IACS member societies.
  • Port state control enforcement of IMO cyber guidance uses ISM Code provisions — vessels without Cyber Risk Assessments, documented incident response procedures, or crew training may receive ISM Code deficiencies during PSC inspection.
  • A compliance alignment table mapping IMO Circ.3 elements to E26 requirements and SMS sections is the most efficient tool for demonstrating integrated compliance to class surveyors, flag state auditors, and PSC officers.
  • IMO's regulatory pipeline includes dedicated SOLAS cyber provisions (potentially 2028-2032) and mandatory cyber incident reporting — shipping companies should build compliance programmes that will accommodate these future mandatory requirements.
