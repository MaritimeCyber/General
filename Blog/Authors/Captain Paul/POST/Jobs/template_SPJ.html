PART 2 · Lesson 6 Cyber Attacks Social Engineering

Phishing & Social Engineering

Social Engineering · Phishing · Spear Phishing · Whaling · Vishing · Smishing · Credential Harvesting · Pretexting · Business Email Compromise — the human attack surface that no firewall can fully block.

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27
September 2026
🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can define social engineering and explain why it is effective, identify the key phishing variants (phishing, spear phishing, whaling, vishing, smishing), explain how credential harvesting and pretexting work, recognise Business Email Compromise (BEC) patterns, and describe the technical and procedural controls that reduce susceptibility — with direct application to maritime crew and shore-based operations.


In late 2023, a crew member aboard a containership received an email that appeared to come from the vessel's crewing agency. The message informed him that his employment contract had been renewed and asked him to log in to a new crew portal to confirm his bank details for salary payments. The link in the email led to a site that looked identical to the real crewing agency portal. He entered his username and password. Within minutes, those credentials were used to log in to the real portal and redirect his next three months of salary to an account controlled by the attackers.

No malware was used. No vulnerability was exploited. No firewall rule was bypassed. The attacker simply sent a convincing email and waited for a human to act on it. This is social engineering — and it is, by a significant margin, the most common entry point for cyberattacks across all industries, including maritime.

The term "social engineering" in security refers to the use of psychological manipulation to cause a person to take an action or reveal information that benefits the attacker. Unlike technical exploits that target software flaws, social engineering targets the one element that no patch can fully fix: human behaviour. People are helpful by nature; they respect authority; they respond to urgency; they trust familiar names. Social engineers exploit all of these instincts.

This lesson covers the main social engineering attack types, how they work technically and psychologically, why maritime environments create specific vulnerabilities, and what controls — technical and non-technical — can reduce the risk. Part 2 of this series examines attacks from the attacker's perspective, because understanding how an attack works is the prerequisite for building effective defences.

1. Social Engineering — Attacking the Human Layer

Every security model recognises multiple layers of defence: physical security, network controls, endpoint protection, application security, and data security. Social engineering attacks a layer that sits above all of these: the people who operate the systems. When a technical control fails to prevent an attack, it is often because a human was manipulated into providing access, credentials, or information that bypassed the technical layer entirely.

Social engineering works because it exploits cognitive biases — predictable patterns in how humans think and make decisions. Security researchers and practitioners frequently reference Robert Cialdini's six principles of influence as a framework for understanding why social engineering attacks succeed:

Six Psychological Principles Social Engineers Exploit
Principle How Attackers Use It Maritime Example
Authority Impersonate a manager, flag authority, port official, or Class surveyor to compel compliance. Email appearing to come from a Class society demands urgent certificate submission via a link.
Urgency Create time pressure so the target acts without verifying: "respond within 2 hours or your account will be locked." "Port clearance expires tonight — update crew manifest credentials immediately."
Social Proof Imply that others have already complied: "all other vessels in the fleet have completed this update." Fake software update notification claiming the fleet has already been upgraded.
Familiarity Use names, logos, and formatting that look familiar and trusted. Reference real projects or colleagues by name. Email with the crewing agency's exact logo and the Chief Officer's correct name in the greeting.
Scarcity Suggest that a resource or opportunity is limited, causing the target to act quickly without scrutiny. "Only 3 berths available at this port — confirm booking now via this link."
Reciprocity Offer something of value first (a document, a tool, a favour) to create a sense of obligation to comply with a follow-on request. Free "maritime cyber assessment template" attached to an email that installs a keylogger when opened.

These principles are not unique to cybercrime — they are the same techniques used in sales, negotiation, and persuasion. Social engineers simply apply them in a context designed to extract credentials or access. Understanding them is the first step toward recognising when you are being manipulated.

2. Phishing Variants — From Mass Campaigns to Targeted Attacks

Phishing is the use of fraudulent communications — almost always email, but increasingly SMS and voice — to deceive a recipient into taking an action that benefits the attacker. That action may be clicking a link that installs malware, submitting credentials to a fake site, opening a malicious attachment, or transferring funds. The term derives from "fishing" — casting a wide net and seeing what gets caught.

🎣

Phishing (Mass)

Generic, high-volume campaigns sent to thousands of addresses. The email appears to come from a bank, courier, tax authority, or widely used service. Success relies on statistical probability — even a 0.1% click rate across 100,000 emails yields 100 compromised users.

Tell-tale signs: generic greeting ("Dear Customer"), mismatched sender domain, urgency, poor grammar in older campaigns.
🎯

Spear Phishing (Targeted)

Highly personalised attacks targeting a specific individual or organisation. The attacker researches the target — their role, colleagues, current projects, communication style — and crafts a message that appears authentic. Success rates are dramatically higher than mass phishing.

Maritime context: A Chief Officer receives an email "from the technical superintendent" asking for login credentials to the port ISM management system before arrival.
🐋

Whaling (Executive Targeting)

Spear phishing directed specifically at senior executives — CEOs, CFOs, Managing Directors. Because these individuals have high authority and often bypass normal approval processes, a successful attack can yield large fraudulent transfers. Whaling often precedes BEC attacks.

Maritime context: Email to the fleet operations director appearing to come from the company CEO requesting an urgent wire transfer for a vessel dry-docking deposit.
📞

Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Phone-based social engineering. The attacker calls the target impersonating IT support, a bank, a government authority, or a colleague. Caller ID spoofing makes the call appear to come from a legitimate number. Vishing is effective because voice communication creates a real-time social pressure that email does not.

Common script: "Hi, this is IT security — we've detected unusual login activity on your account. I need to verify your credentials to protect your account now."
📱

Smishing (SMS Phishing)

SMS-based phishing. Fraudulent text messages impersonate couriers, banks, or government services to direct the recipient to a malicious link or prompt them to call a fraudulent number. Smishing links often lead to mobile-optimised credential harvesting pages.

Maritime crew risk: Crew members at port receive SMS messages claiming to be from port immigration or customs requiring immediate document submission via a link.

3. How Phishing Works — The Technical Mechanics

Understanding the technical mechanisms behind phishing explains both why it succeeds and where defences can be placed. A phishing attack typically has four technical components: email spoofing, credential harvesting infrastructure, social proof elements, and exfiltration.

Step-by-Step: A Credential Harvesting Attack
1
Reconnaissance

Attacker researches the target using LinkedIn, company websites, OSINT tools, and social media. They identify the target's role, manager, colleagues, and current projects. For maritime targets, vessel tracking sites, port authority announcements, and crew lists may be used.

2
Domain Spoofing / Lookalike Domain

Attacker registers a lookalike domain: crewingagency-portal.com instead of crewingagency.com. Alternatively, they use email header manipulation to make the displayed "From" address appear legitimate while the actual sending domain differs. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records are checked — if not configured, the spoofed email passes basic filters.

3
Phishing Page Infrastructure

A cloned login page is hosted on the attacker's server. Modern phishing kits copy the entire HTML/CSS of the legitimate site, including images, fonts, and the favicon. Many phishing pages now use HTTPS with a valid TLS certificate — the padlock icon no longer indicates a safe site. Evilginx and similar tools can proxy the real site in real time, defeating MFA by capturing session tokens.

4
Credential Capture & Exfiltration

When the target submits credentials on the fake page, the credentials are immediately sent to the attacker (via Telegram bot, email, or a backend database). The page then redirects the victim to the real login page, often showing a "password incorrect" message — ensuring the victim tries again on the real site and does not immediately notice the attack.

5
Access & Lateral Movement

The stolen credentials are immediately tested against other services (credential stuffing — most people reuse passwords). Access to the compromised account allows the attacker to read email, impersonate the victim in further attacks, or escalate privileges if the account has administrative access.

⚠️ Why HTTPS Does Not Mean Safe

A common misconception is that a padlock icon in the browser address bar means a site is safe. HTTPS only means the connection between your browser and the server is encrypted — it says nothing about whether the server itself is malicious. Phishing sites routinely obtain free TLS certificates from services like Let's Encrypt. Always verify the full domain name, not just the padlock.

4. Pretexting and Business Email Compromise (BEC)

Pretexting is the creation of a fabricated scenario — a "pretext" — to establish false trust and extract information or access. Unlike a simple phishing email, pretexting involves building a sustained, convincing persona. An attacker might call a vessel operator posing as an IT vendor, establish a relationship over several calls, and eventually request VPN credentials to "perform a scheduled system update."

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is one of the most financially damaging forms of social engineering. The FBI's IC3 report consistently rates BEC as the cybercrime category with the highest total financial losses — billions of dollars annually. BEC occurs when an attacker either gains access to a legitimate business email account (through phishing or credential theft) or creates a convincing lookalike account, then uses it to redirect payments, change banking details, or authorise fraudulent transactions.

Common BEC Attack Patterns in Maritime
CEO Fraud Attacker impersonates the CEO and emails the CFO requesting an urgent wire transfer for a "confidential acquisition" or vessel purchase. The instruction emphasises confidentiality and speed, bypassing normal approval workflows.
Vendor/Supplier Fraud Attacker compromises a ship chandler's or bunkering company's email account and sends the vessel operator an invoice with changed banking details. The email looks authentic because it comes from the real vendor's account.
Port Agent Fraud Lookalike email from a port agent requests advance port disbursement account (PDA) payment to a new bank account. Given the tight timelines of port operations, crews and operators often pay without verifying the account change.
Crew Salary Diversion Attacker impersonates a crew member and emails the crewing department requesting a change of bank account for salary payments. The request appears legitimate and is processed without secondary verification.

5. Detection and Defence

Defending against social engineering requires layered controls — no single measure is sufficient. The most effective approach combines technical email security controls with procedural verification requirements and ongoing awareness training.

🔧 Technical Controls
  • SPF — Sender Policy Framework: DNS record specifying which mail servers can send email for a domain. Reduces domain spoofing.
  • DKIM — DomainKeys Identified Mail: cryptographic signature on emails, allowing the receiving server to verify the sender.
  • DMARC — Domain-based Message Authentication: policy that tells receiving servers what to do when SPF/DKIM fail (reject, quarantine).
  • Anti-phishing email gateway — scans links and attachments, rewrites URLs for safe browsing analysis.
  • MFA / Phishing-resistant MFA — FIDO2/WebAuthn hardware keys are the most phishing-resistant second factor (resistant even to real-time proxy attacks).
  • Email banners — label external email with a visible warning banner.
📋 Procedural Controls
  • Out-of-band verification — any request to change bank account details or authorise unusual payments must be verified via a separate, known phone number (not one provided in the suspicious email).
  • Payment approval workflow — two-person authorisation for transfers above a threshold; no single-person override.
  • New vendor/banking change checklist — structured checklist for any change to payment details.
  • Phishing simulation exercises — regular simulated phishing campaigns to measure and improve awareness.
  • Reporting culture — clear, easy process for crew and staff to report suspected phishing without fear of blame.
🔍 Detection Indicators
  • Sender domain differs from the display name domain.
  • Hover over links reveals a different URL from the displayed text.
  • Unexpected urgency, especially combined with a request to bypass normal process.
  • Requests for credentials, payment changes, or sensitive data via email.
  • Generic greetings in what claims to be a personalised message.
  • Login page URL that does not exactly match the legitimate domain.
  • Email received from someone you know but asking for something unusual — verify via phone.
✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

During an E26 CRSI assessment, the human awareness component consistently reveals the same pattern: crew and shore staff who are technically competent in their maritime roles have had almost no exposure to cybersecurity awareness training. During a simulated phishing exercise conducted as part of an assessment, over 60% of the crew members tested clicked the link in a simulated phishing email — and of those, more than half entered credentials on the fake login page.

This is not a reflection of low intelligence. It is a reflection of context. A bulk carrier crew member managing a full watch schedule, maintenance records, and port clearance documentation simultaneously does not have the mental bandwidth to scrutinise every email. Attackers know this. They time their phishing campaigns to coincide with port arrivals — when crews are busy, distracted, and under pressure.

The most effective countermeasure I have seen is not a spam filter or an email banner — it is a simple, clearly communicated procedure: any request to change payment details, account information, or access credentials must be verified by phone using a number already in company records, never a number provided in the email. This single procedural control, consistently enforced, prevents the most financially damaging BEC attacks. The technology is secondary to the procedure.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 6
  • Social engineering attacks the human layer — no technical patch can fully prevent it.
  • Phishing variants range from mass campaigns (email) to targeted spear phishing and whaling, plus voice (vishing) and SMS (smishing) channels.
  • Credential harvesting pages mimic legitimate sites exactly; HTTPS/padlock does not indicate safety.
  • BEC is one of the highest-loss cybercrime categories and is particularly relevant in maritime (port payments, crew salary, vendor invoices).
  • SPF, DKIM, and DMARC reduce email spoofing; phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2) prevents credential theft from being usable.
  • Procedural out-of-band verification for payment changes is the single most effective BEC countermeasure.
  • Regular phishing simulation exercises and a blame-free reporting culture are essential human-layer controls.
