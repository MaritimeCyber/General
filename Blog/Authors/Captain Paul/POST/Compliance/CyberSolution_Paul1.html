2. Why Was Mythos Created — and Why Is It Dangerous?

Anthropic built Mythos to be the ultimate software engineering AI — capable of reading, writing, and debugging complex codebases autonomously. It succeeded. And in doing so, it became equally capable of finding security vulnerabilities that no human or tool had ever found before.

⚠️ The Dual-Use Problem

A model that autonomously understands and writes code at expert level can also autonomously find, analyze, and exploit vulnerabilities in that code. Anthropic recognized this and created Project Glasswing — restricting access to an 11-company consortium of verified partners, providing $100M in usage credits and $4M in open-source security donations for defensive use only.

Anthropic became the first major AI company to publicly state that a model they built was too dangerous to release to the general public.

3. How Mythos Attacks — The Autonomous Exploit Pipeline

Traditional hacking requires a human to guide each step: find a vulnerability, understand it, write code to exploit it, test, iterate. This takes days to weeks per target.

Mythos does this autonomously. Given a single prompt, it decomposes the objective into sub-goals and executes them in parallel — scanning code, identifying weaknesses, writing exploit code, testing, refining — without any human in the loop. It runs 24/7 across thousands of parallel instances at marginal cost.

Rather than stopping there, it identified a workaround for a missing NFSv4 feature and constructed a 20-step Return-Oriented Programming (ROP) chain to escalate from network access to full system control — without a single line of human-written exploit code.

Part 2 — The Attack Surface
Why Ships Are More Exposed, Not Less

6. IT and OT — Two Worlds Inside One Hull

IT (Information Technology) is the computing environment we use every day — email, documents, the internet, business systems. When something breaks, you restart or patch it.

OT (Operational Technology) directly controls physical equipment. On a vessel: propulsion engines, generator management, ballast pumps, cargo handling. When an OT system fails, it is not a software error — it is a safety emergency. The ship can deviate from course, the engine can stop.

Many OT systems across energy, transport, and maritime are decades old — warranties expired, security patches impossible to apply. Designed for reliability, not security. (Bain & Company) Vessels are where these two worlds coexist in a single hull — with a boundary far thinner than most assume.

7. Three Reasons Ships Are Especially Exposed

⏳ Patch Cycles Tied to Drydock (1–5 Years)

Core OT systems on vessels cannot be patched while underway. The next drydock opportunity may be one to five years away. In an environment where AI compresses vulnerability discovery to near-zero time, a 1–5 year patch gap is an open window. (CSA Lab Space)

📶 Remote Operations Are Expanding the Attack Surface

Smart ship technology and remote monitoring are widening the data connection between vessels and shore. This is the right direction for efficiency — but it proportionally enlarges the surface accessible to external attackers.

🚨 No Dedicated Security Staff Onboard

A large shipping company’s cybersecurity team is at headquarters. When an attack is underway, is there anyone aboard who can respond immediately? On most vessels, the answer is no.

8. This Already Happened — The 2025 AI Espionage Campaign

In November 2025, suspected Chinese state-sponsored actors jailbroke Claude Code and ran an autonomous cyber espionage campaign against approximately 30 global organizations. AI agents autonomously performed 80–90% of all operations — reconnaissance, privilege escalation, lateral movement, credential theft, data exfiltration — at a speed no human operator could match. This campaign used a pre-Mythos model. The shipping and shipbuilding industry is traditionally one of the primary targets of state-sponsored cyberattacks, alongside tech companies, financial institutions, and government agencies.

87% of global organizations report experiencing AI-powered cyberattacks in the past year. (Bain & Company) This is not a future threat. It is happening now.

Part 3 — The Defense
When Algorithms Attack a Ship, What Are Our Weapons?

9. The First Principle — Defense in Depth

More than 99% of vulnerabilities Mythos discovers are not in any CVE database. This means the defense approach must change — from “eliminate known vulnerabilities” to “build a structure where vulnerabilities are hard to reach, and damage is minimized even if they are reached.” (ArmorCode)

💡 Defense in Depth

A layered structure where the next defense line holds even if one line is breached. Mythos may successfully gain entry — but gets blocked at the next stage. Network segmentation, access controls, anomaly detection — these are defense lines that exist before CVE, and remain effective against AI-powered attackers. (Bain & Company)

10. SFI-Based CBS Asset Classification

Defense begins with knowing what needs protecting. SFI (Ships Form Index) is the international standard code system for all vessel systems (720 = ECDIS, 730 = AIS, 411 = propulsion). CBS (Computer Based System), defined in IACS UR E26, covers all computer-based systems onboard. Combining both creates an SFI-coded CBS asset inventory — a hierarchical risk map of every cyber-relevant asset. Each CBS node receives three parameters:

SFI-Based CBS Risk Score Matrix
SFI System CIA Priority Patch Access Network Exposure Risk

720 ECDIS Navigation Integrity: HIGH Drydock only Via IT + update port HIGH
746 Satellite Comms (VSAT) Confidentiality: HIGH Remote-updateable Internet-facing HIGH
520 Cargo Mgmt System Confidentiality: MED Remote-updateable Via IT network MEDIUM
820 CCTV / IoT Sensors Availability: LOW Remote-updateable Via IT network ENTRY POINT

CIA = Confidentiality / Integrity / Availability. Risk Score = CIA priority × Patch accessibility × Network exposure.

11. 5-Step Defense Execution Framework

The diagnostic tools above integrate into actionable steps — a sequence maritime practitioners can apply in the field.

Mythos-Era 5-Step Defense Framework
1
Asset Inventory

Maintain SFI-coded CBS inventory in current state. Explicitly identify all IT–OT interface nodes: satellite comms gateway, remote monitoring servers, USB connection ports.

2
Risk Prioritization

Assign risk scores to each CBS. Prioritize Mythos’s preferred entry points: remotely accessible services, legacy operating systems, IT–OT bridge nodes.

3
AI-Assisted Scanning

Use frontier AI models for pre-drydock vulnerability diagnosis. Project Glasswing partners with Mythos Preview access can run autonomous vulnerability searches at attacker-equivalent scale. (CSA Lab Space)

4
Compensating Controls

For legacy CBS that cannot be patched pending drydock: VLAN redesign to isolate the CBS, detection for abnormal traffic to that segment. Block access instead of patching.

5
Class Survey Integration

Integrate AI scan results and updated SFI asset inventory into IACS E27 Annual Survey submissions. Class surveyors’ records must reflect Mythos-era threat perspective.

12. Three Core Defense Principles

🛡️ Don’t only defend what you know.

CVE-unregistered vulnerabilities are more dangerous than listed ones. Network segmentation, access controls, and least privilege are defense lines that exist before any CVE database — and they work.

🔧 Separate patch cycles from drydock.

Software CBS that can be updated remotely should follow enterprise-level patch cycles. Hardware CBS with low patch accessibility should have compensating controls applied instead of waiting for drydock.

🤖 Use AI for defense too.

Scan your own systems before attackers do, using the same AI tools. AISI confirmed: Mythos can autonomously attack weakly defended networks but could not make the same determination for well-defended environments with active defenders and anomaly detection. (UK AISI)

The maritime market is no exception to AI hacking.

SFI codes to map assets. Risk scores to set priorities. Patching independent of drydock. Network isolation for legacy systems. AI scan results in annual class submissions. Working systems, not reports.
