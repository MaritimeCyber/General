In China, The Limitations of Shipyard-Submitted Documents (1): Vessel Asset Inventory

First : Vessel Asset Inventory [E26, 5.1.3]

※ The shipyard and SI company names are anonymized (H*, M*).

Jack
Superintendent · MSC (Mediterranean Shipping Company)

I want to share some of the problems I have experienced with cyber security documents for ships being built in China. The first document I want to talk about is the Vessel Asset Inventory under E26, Section 5.1.3.

1What the Shipyard Submitted

A vessel is currently under construction at H*, one of the largest shipyards in China. M* is responsible for the cyber security SI work. They are known as one of the most technically advanced SI companies in China.

So, what did shipyard submit for approval?

Just three pages, with around 50 entries with so many pending.

Yes, only three pages for the Vessel Asset Inventory. Surprised.

Of course, fewer pages do not always mean a bad document. But the assets on board must be properly identified, and the important information about each system and its connections must be included.

However, the document I received was little more than a list of system names. In my view, it was nowhere near sufficient to provide a proper understanding of the vessel's cyber-related assets or to support the subsequent risk assessment and selection of appropriate security measures.

2"What Is the Problem?"

I raised the following question with the shipyard:

"Do you really consider this a reliable document that adequately identifies the cyber-related assets on board? Is this why you submitted it for approval?"

The shipyard's answer was quite straightforward:

"This document was prepared by M*, one of the leading cyber security SI companies in China. We simply submitted what they provided. What is the problem?"

OMG. Really? Is that supposed to be enough?

Just because a company is well known, does that mean its documents are always good? Is that really a reason to approve them?

My decision was clear.

REJECT. The problem was not the number of pages. The problem was that the document did not meet the basic requirements for properly identifying and managing the vessel's cyber-related assets.

What is even more surprising is that the shipyard did not seem to understand what was wrong with the document. They simply forwarded the material they had received from the SI company to the shipowner without any proper review.

To make matters worse, some of my fellow colleagues on the owner's side also seem unsure whether the document is acceptable or completely inadequate, or whether they should approve it as submitted or raise comments.

3A Vessel Asset Inventory Is Not Just a List of System Names

A Vessel Asset Inventory is not just a list of system names. It should help us understand what assets are installed on board, how they are connected, and which assets need to be considered from a cyber security perspective. It is one of the basic documents needed to support the risk assessment and the development of appropriate security measures.

If the inventory itself is incomplete or unreliable, how can we have confidence in the risk assessment and other cyber security documents built on top of it?

4To Shipowners and Classification Societies

To shipowners and classification societies involved in newbuilding projects in China, I would like to make one point very clear.

Do not approve cyber security documents simply because they have been submitted by the shipyard or prepared by a well-known SI company.

Check whether the documents properly reflect the actual vessel, whether the required technical information is included, and whether the contents are consistent with the other cyber security documents. If something is missing or inadequate, raise your comments and ask for proper revisions.

You may also receive a Vessel Asset Inventory that is only three pages long.

If such a document is approved without a proper technical review, important asset information may remain unidentified or undocumented. This can create serious problems later when assessing cyber risks and determining the security measures needed to protect the vessel.

Approval should not be just a formality. Someone needs to check the document properly and make sure it meets the actual requirements.

Our responsibility does not end when we put an approval stamp on a document. If the document has not been properly reviewed, its shortcomings may only become apparent later, when the vessel is in operation.

This is the first issue I have encountered while reviewing cyber security documentation for ships being built in China.

I have a strong message for Chinese shipyards and shipowners: Hire a competent cyber security SI company of Korea.

More to come.
