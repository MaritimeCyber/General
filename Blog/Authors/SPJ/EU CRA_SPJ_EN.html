EU CRA – Application to theft and incident reporting (September 11, 2026)

Regulatory explanation and comparison with IACS UR E27

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

The EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) sets cybersecurity requirements for "products with digital elements (PDE)" — including both hardware and software — as a legal condition for placing them on the EU market. It originated from the recognition that post-launch security levels had been left entirely to manufacturers' discretion, and that users had no reliable way to judge a product's security — and it addresses this through manufacturer obligations spanning the product's entire lifecycle.

Ⅰ. Overview of the EU CRA

The implementation timeline has three stages.

  • 1 Dec 10, 2024: Entry into force
  • 2 Sep 11, 2026: Manufacturers' vulnerability/incident reporting obligation applies (already in effect)
  • 3 Dec 11, 2027: Full application (essential requirements, conformity assessment, CE marking)

The obligated party is the manufacturer, though importers and distributors also carry role-specific obligations. Since the Regulation applies to any product placed on the EU market regardless of the manufacturer's location, Korean equipment suppliers are directly in scope the moment they supply an EU shipowner or shipyard. Fines for violating essential requirements can reach up to €15 million or 2.5% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Products are classified by risk level, and products such as firewalls, intrusion detection systems, network management systems, and industrial control-function products are likely to fall under "important products" (Annex III) — something the shipping and shipbuilding sector should watch closely.

Ⅱ. The CRA and the Vessels It Applies To

The CRA does not regulate the vessel itself — it applies to the individual products with digital elements (PDE) installed on board. Since a vessel is a system made up of these products combined, whether each component complies with the CRA becomes a central procurement issue.

Excluded: MED-scoped equipment. Marine equipment within the scope of the Marine Equipment Directive (Directive 2014/90/EU, MED) is excluded from the CRA (Art. 2(3)) and continues to be governed by the existing MED certification regime and IACS rules.

Applicable: non-MED products. The following products fall under the CRA once placed on the EU market.

  • Remote-access gateways, VPN equipment, firewalls, industrial switches/routers
  • OT sensors and gateways for cargo, machinery, and equipment monitoring
  • Commercial operating systems, virtualization platforms, management software
  • Communication and data-collection equipment developed for maritime use that does not fall under MED items

So the first step in any vessel project is to sort onboard equipment into MED / non-MED, and for each non-MED product, confirm its CRA classification and the manufacturer's compliance status. Whether components of a vessel built outside the EU and delivered to an EU shipowner count as being "placed on the market" needs case-by-case judgment, and should be confirmed against EU authority guidance and legal review.

Ⅲ. How the CRA Differs From IACS UR E27

The two frameworks are not competitors — they are complementary. E27 is the standard by which class societies certify the cyber resilience of onboard systems and equipment; the CRA is the law that determines a product's access to the EU market.

The biggest difference is timing and the scope of responsibility. E27 is largely about confirming a system's security performance at the point of delivery, while the CRA extends manufacturer responsibility to vulnerability management and reporting throughout the post-launch support period. That said, E27's development, configuration-management, and security-update requirements — and its IEC 62443-4-1/4-2 evidence — can be reused for CRA compliance, so designing a cross-mapping table from the start is the way to reduce the double burden.

Ⅳ. The CRA's Three Core Pillars and Their Impact on Shipbuilding & Maritime

①Security Built In at the Design Stage (Security by Design / by Default)

A product must have no known, exploitable vulnerabilities at the time of release, and its default configuration must be secure. It must provide access control, data confidentiality/integrity protection, attack-surface minimization, logging, and secure data deletion.

Impact on shipbuilding & maritime: The practice of delivering equipment with default passwords left in place or unnecessary ports left open is no longer acceptable. Checking security configuration during commissioning and delivery becomes a mandatory step.

②Lifecycle Vulnerability Management and Reporting

For the support period (in principle a minimum of five years, or the expected usage period if shorter), manufacturers must identify and document vulnerabilities, provide security updates free of charge, and operate a coordinated vulnerability disclosure (CVD) policy.

Impact on shipbuilding & maritime: A vessel's operating life is 25–30 years — a large gap against typical equipment support periods. Replacement-equipment plans, isolation, and compensating controls for after support ends need to be worked out at the CSDD stage.

③Supply-Chain Transparency and Demonstrating Conformity

Manufacturers must demonstrate compliance through an SBOM, technical documentation, an EU Declaration of Conformity (DoC), and CE marking, and are also obligated to perform due diligence on commercial and open-source components.

Impact on shipbuilding & maritime: Shipyards and shipowners need to build DoC submission, SBOM submission, and security-update-policy requirements into procurement contracts — which connects directly to E26 asset-inventory management.

Ⅴ. Vulnerability Reporting, SBOM, SbD, and Real-Time Threat Response

(1) Vulnerability and Incident Reporting Obligation

Once a manufacturer becomes aware of an "actively exploited vulnerability" or an "incident with a significant impact on security," it must report in the following order.

  • 1 Within 24 hours: early warning
  • 2 Within 72 hours: vulnerability/incident notification (details, impact, mitigation measures)
  • 3 Final report: for a vulnerability, within 14 days from when a corrective action becomes available; for an incident, within one month of notification

Reports are submitted through the single reporting platform operated by ENISA and forwarded to the designated CSIRT. Since the obligation sits with the manufacturer, the role of shipowners/managers and shipyards is to put in place a system for receiving manufacturer notifications and an operational-side reporting framework (with a separate timeline under NIS2 where applicable).

(2) SBOM

At minimum it must cover the product's top-level dependencies and be produced in a machine-readable format such as SPDX or CycloneDX. It's part of the technical documentation and there is no obligation to publish it, but it must be submitted on request from market-surveillance authorities. In practice, this needs to be linked to a vulnerability database so that affected products can be automatically identified whenever a component vulnerability is disclosed.

(3) The SbD Process

Threat modeling and risk assessment must be carried out at the design stage, and the results mapped to the Annex I requirements and recorded in the technical documentation. Companies that have already adopted IEC 62443-4-1 (Secure Development Lifecycle) can reuse their evidence for requirements definition, secure design, testing, defect management, and update management.

(4) Real-Time Threat Response Process

The CRA doesn't use the term "real-time response," but the 24-hour early-warning obligation effectively presupposes continuous monitoring and a rapid triage framework. The elements needed to build this out are:

  • Operating a manufacturer PSIRT and an external-report intake channel (CVD)
  • Automated impact analysis built on SBOM/vulnerability-database integration
  • Criteria for determining active exploitation and reporting thresholds
  • Escalation paths and contact structure across vessel ↔ shore-side fleet management ↔ manufacturer PSIRT
  • Security patch distribution, integrity verification, and rollback procedures (linked to SCARP)

Conclusion

The CRA adds "post-delivery product lifecycle responsibility" on top of the "security at the point of delivery" that E26/E27 have addressed. The response can be summarized in three points.

  • 1 Classify onboard equipment as MED / non-MED and identify which products fall under the CRA.
  • 2 Build DoC, SBOM, update-policy, and incident-notification requirements into procurement contracts.
  • 3 Link manufacturer-notification frameworks and end-of-support plans into the CSDD and SCARP.

※ Delegated acts, harmonised standards, and Commission guidance continue to be updated, so please confirm the latest details against official EU sources before final application.

⚓ Captain Paul's Take

Until now, IACS UR E27 has been, from a supplier's point of view, something close to "a job that's done once you're certified." The goal was the moment you received the Type Approval document, and vulnerability management and incident response after that tended to be comparatively loose. The EU CRA is saying this mindset has to change fundamentally: certification isn't the end, it's the start — and as long as a product stays on the market, security responsibility stays with it too. A supplier that hasn't built out a PSIRT, a CVD policy, and a 24-hour early-warning framework can lose out in procurement on readiness alone.

But there is no reason to see this purely as a burden. Shipyard procurement teams have already started asking about UR E27 Type Approval status as early as the RFQ stage. Suppliers who get ahead on the CRA's vulnerability-reporting, SBOM, and lifecycle-management requirements are the ones who get to be remembered by shipowners and shipyards first as a "ready supplier." For companies quick to make that mindset shift, regulation stops being a barrier to entry and becomes a competitive edge.
