Article 2 — Zones and Conduit Diagram. Conceptual cover illustration.
This article follows Article 1: The Cyber Resilience System Integrator and the Six Core Ship-Level Deliverables and examines the first deliverable: the Zones and conduit diagram.

The Zones and Conduit Diagram, or ZCD, is the principal ship-level document used to demonstrate how computer-based systems (CBSs) are grouped into security zones, how communication is permitted within and across those zones, and how interfaces with networks outside the IACS UR E26 scope are controlled. A reliable ZCD is not merely a network drawing: it is the controlled architectural baseline linking supplier information, vessel design, commissioning evidence and operation-phase maintenance.

Key message: A compliant ZCD must represent the same in-scope CBSs as the Vessel Asset Inventory, show their zone allocation and physical locations, and agree with the communication and boundary-control design in the Cyber Security Design Description (CSDD) and the final installed vessel. It must also remain maintainable after delivery, because the Shipowner is required to keep the diagram updated and demonstrate continued management of security-zone boundaries during the operational survey cycle.

Terminology and interpretation note

How This Article Uses “ZCD,” “Conduit” and Related Terms

Series scope. The six core deliverables group the five document categories in UR E26 §5.1 and the test procedure in §5.2.1. The exclusion assessment and description of compensating countermeasures apply where relevant. Supplier documentation, test records and the Shipowner’s operation-phase program are also part of the compliance framework.

CRSI means Cyber Resilience System Integrator in this series: a specialist performing or supporting the cyber-resilience aspects of the IACS-defined Systems Integrator function. CRSI is not a separate IACS stakeholder category. Responsibilities follow the applicable requirements and the party’s appointment.

Applicability. UR E26 Rev.1 is implemented by IACS Societies for ships within its scope contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2024. It may serve as non-mandatory guidance for other ships. The applicable Class Rules, implementation dates and project conditions must be identified for each vessel; the newest published edition is not automatically the applicable project edition.

IACS UR E26 uses the document title “Zones and conduit diagram.” This article uses the abbreviation ZCD for readability. UR E26 formally defines a security zone, a network segment, a logical network segment, a physical network segment and an untrusted network. It does not provide a separate formal definition of conduit or zone boundary device in Section 2.

For the ZCD requirement, UR E26 expressly uses the term conduit for network communication between different security zones and for any communication between a security zone and an untrusted network. IEC 62443 uses a broader supporting concept in which a conduit groups communication channels connecting zones under a common security treatment. That IEC concept may support the engineering method, but it should not be presented as the formal IACS definition.

IACS UR E26 does not prescribe a mandatory number of ZCD sheets or require separately titled “Physical ZCD” and “Logical ZCD” drawings. Separate coordinated views are recommended for complex vessels, while a combined representation may be used where all required information remains clear, complete and acceptable to the Society. A Purdue Model overlay may also be used for explanation, but it is not an IACS requirement and does not replace the UR E26 zone and conduit decisions.

1. Why the Zones and Conduit Diagram Matters

A conventional network drawing may show switches, cables, IP networks and connected equipment. A ZCD must go further by explaining the vessel’s security architecture. It must identify the in-scope CBSs, allocate them to security zones, show the relevant physical locations, illustrate required communication relationships and make boundary-crossing communication traceable to the controls described in the CSDD.

The ZCD serves three related functions:

  1. Architectural definition: it establishes the vessel-level segmentation model used to limit cyber-incident propagation and unauthorised lateral movement.
  2. Design traceability: it provides the architectural basis for the CSDD, which explains communication purpose, protocols, data flows, boundary devices and permitted traffic.
  3. Lifecycle control: it becomes an as-built baseline that the Shipowner must maintain under Management of Change and use during the operational survey cycle.

Under UR E26, all CBSs within scope are grouped into security zones with defined security policies and capabilities. Zones are either isolated or connected through means that control the communicated data, and only explicitly authorised traffic may cross a security-zone boundary.

Regulatory reference: IACS UR E26 Rev.1, §§4.2.1.1–4.2.1.3.

2. Regulatory Purpose and Mandatory Content

2.1 Security-Zone Requirement

UR E26 defines a security zone as a collection of in-scope CBSs that meet the same security requirements and are subject to an applicable access-control policy. This means that a security zone is not automatically equivalent to one Supplier system, one shipboard department, one room, one switch, one VLAN, one IP subnet or one Purdue level.

Those attributes may influence the design, but the zone decision must be defensible in terms of common security requirements, trust, exposure, access policy, functional dependencies and the ability to control boundary-crossing communication.

Regulatory reference: IACS UR E26 Rev.1, §2, definition of “Security zone,” and §4.2.1.3.

2.2 Minimum ZCD Content

The Systems Integrator is required to submit the ZCD and CSDD during the design phase. The ZCD must include the following information.

CRSI recommended practice

Grouped Representation Must Remain Complete

A complex ZCD may group same-zone communication to preserve readability, but only where the underlying communication relationships remain complete and traceable through a controlled communication register or approved Supplier topology reference. Aggregation should not conceal an undocumented path or omit a relationship needed to understand the zone policy.

Interpretation boundary

Inter-Zone Non-IP Communication

UR E26 §4.2.1.4.1 requires network communication between in-scope zones and any communication with untrusted networks to be illustrated. “Non-IP” is not a blanket exemption: distinguish a non-IP network from a point-to-point serial or discrete interface. ClassNK’s Edition 1.1 guidance (§4-2.2) describes same-zone and inter-zone communication in IP terms and separately addresses serial and discrete communication with untrusted networks. This is a class implementation explanation, not a universal redefinition of “network” in UR E26.

For other inter-zone links, document the protocol, direction and boundary-control or isolation implications, and agree the representation with the Society. Showing boundary-crossing serial, fieldbus and discrete links is recommended to make functional dependencies visible. Communication with an untrusted network must be illustrated regardless of whether it uses IP.

5. Zone Design and Segmentation

5.1 Zone-Allocation Criteria

Correct zone allocation requires more than grouping systems by equipment name or physical location. A CRSI should assess the following characteristics and document the rationale for the chosen grouping.

Safety function and consequenceWhether compromise or failure could affect human safety, vessel safety or the environment.
System categoryThe UR E22 category and associated integrity, availability and segregation expectations, where applicable.

IACS requirement

Local Control and Monitoring Must Be Addressed in the Zone Model

For CBSs needed for local backup control under SOLAS II-1 Regulation 31, including the HMI needed for effective local operation, UR E26 §4.4.2 requires independence from the primary control system. The local control and monitoring CBS must be self-contained for its intended operation. Where network communication is arranged with a remote-control system or another CBS, the applicable segmentation and network-protection safeguards apply and the local control and monitoring system is treated as a separate security zone. Different design concepts may receive case-by-case consideration from the Society.

5.3 Physical and Logical Segmentation

Figure 2. Physical and logical segmentation. Both require controlled boundary communication; logical segmentation is used only where physical segmentation is not required.

ClassNK explains physical segmentation as the use of separate Layer 2 network components without sharing the same Layer 2 switch. Logical segmentation may use VLANs or other logical mechanisms on shared infrastructure, provided that communication crossing the zones is redirected through the applicable boundary-control function.

ClassNK Q&A Q99 clarifies that a single firewall can serve separate physical segments when its functions and configuration keep the relevant physical ports independent. VLAN separation over one shared physical port does not meet this physical-segmentation interpretation. This is ClassNK guidance; verify the arrangement with the vessel’s Society.

Class implementation guidance: ClassNK, Guidelines for Cyber Resilience of Ships, Edition 1.1, Chapter 4, §4-2; UR E26/27 Q&A, Q99.
Interpretation caution

A Router or Layer 3 Switch Is Not Automatically Equivalent to a Firewall

UR E26 identifies routers as one example of a means that may control data communicated between zones. It separately requires security zones to be protected by firewalls or equivalent means. A router or Layer 3 switch may therefore form part of an accepted boundary solution only where the implemented security functions provide demonstrable protection equivalent to the applicable firewall requirement and the arrangement is accepted by the Society.

Simple Layer 3 forwarding, static routing or basic subnet separation should not be presented as equivalent boundary protection. Supporting evidence should address, as applicable, restrictive traffic policy, prevention of unintended bypass, secure configuration management, logging, denial-of-service considerations, configuration backup, change control and suitability for the availability and safety requirements of the connected zones. These evidence items are CRSI-recommended substantiation, not a separate universal checklist stated verbatim by UR E26.

5.4 DMZ Use

UR E26 does not require a DMZ at every security-zone boundary. A DMZ is an architectural pattern that may be appropriate where direct communication between high-trust OT and lower-trust networks should be avoided, where intermediary services are required, or where remote maintenance and ship-to-shore communication justify an intermediate zone.

Determine the scope of DMZ assets from their functions, interfaces and role in the vessel architecture under UR E26 §1.3.2. A missing E26/E27 capability does not by itself make an asset out of scope: it may require remediation, accepted compensating countermeasures or a justified exclusion. Identify the DMZ assets, services, scope and boundary controls, including any equipment relied upon to meet the vessel’s cyber-resilience requirements.

6. Conduits and Communication Scope

6.1 Communication within a Security Zone

UR E26 requires the ZCD to illustrate network communication between systems within the same security zone. The CSDD then describes the purpose and characteristics of that communication, including relevant protocols and data flows.

Every packet-level flow does not need to be printed individually on the overview diagram. Grouped representation is a recommended documentation method, subject to Society acceptance, where the underlying flow set remains complete, controlled and traceable.

6.2 Communication between Security Zones

Network communication between different security zones is represented as a conduit. The ZCD should show the source and destination zones, the direction where relevant, the communication path, the applicable boundary device or control function and a controlled conduit identifier or reference. The CSDD specifies the communication purpose, protocols, data flows and traffic permitted to cross the boundary.

6.3 Communication with Untrusted Networks

An untrusted network is any network outside the scope of UR E26. Communication between an in-scope security zone and an untrusted network is represented as a conduit. The associated CSDD description must include discrete signals, serial communication and the purpose and characteristics of IP-based network communication, together with boundary devices and permitted traffic.

Regulatory reference: IACS UR E26 Rev.1, §2 and §4.2.1.4.1.

6.5 Excluded CBSs and Untrusted-Network Treatment

Scope consequence

Exclusion status and interface trust classification should be recorded separately, but they are related. Where the Society accepts exclusion of a CBS from the UR E26 scope under §6.4, the resulting scope boundary must be reflected in the design. UR E26 §4.2.1.3 states that systems, networks or CBSs outside the scope are considered untrusted networks.

Communication between such an excluded CBS and an in-scope security zone must be shown as communication with an untrusted system and controlled in accordance with §4.2.1, including the physical-segmentation requirement or the stated alternative where accepted. An exclusion does not remove the boundary obligations of the remaining in-scope zone. Record the exclusion basis and Society decision, and reconcile them with the asset scope and interface documentation.

The ZCD should not become an unreadable substitute for the CSDD or a repository for every IP address, port number and firewall rule. A controlled data-ownership model reduces conflicting duplication. 7.2 Optional Purdue Overlay

A Purdue-level overlay may help explain the functional hierarchy of a complex OT environment. It remains informative only: Purdue levels are not security zones, one level may contain multiple zones, and one zone may span more than one functional level where justified. Project conventions such as “Level 3.5” should be labelled as conventions and must not override UR E26 requirements for safety, wireless, navigation/machinery separation, untrusted networks or zone isolation.

8. Development and As-Built Lifecycle

The ZCD should be developed iteratively from preliminary scope identification through as-built verification. During construction, the Systems Integrator is required to keep the diagram updated. Changes affecting hardware, software, interfaces, segmentation, boundary devices, wireless, remote access or Owner-supplied equipment must be assessed through Management of Change.

Regulatory references: IACS UR E26 Rev.1, §4.2.1.4.2 and §5.1; IACS UR E22 Rev.3 Corr.1, applicable MoC provisions.
Detailed 15-Step ZCD Development Methodology
  1. Establish the preliminary CBS scope. Prepare the preliminary Vessel Asset Inventory and categorisation register.
  2. Assign controlled identifiers. Establish IDs for CBSs, equipment, zones, conduits, segments, boundary devices and external domains.
  3. Collect Supplier architecture information. Obtain asset inventories, physical/logical topologies, interfaces, protocols and remote-access information.
  4. Identify actual interfaces and dependencies. Reconcile Supplier information with vessel wiring, network and location data.
  5. Define zone-design principles. Agree criteria for safety, trust, access policy, exposure, availability, isolation and maintenance.
  6. Develop the preliminary Zone Catalogue. Allocate each CBS to a proposed zone or other approved domain and document the rationale.
  7. Identify network segments and segmentation type. Determine whether each boundary is physical or logical and verify where physical separation is mandatory.
  8. Identify communication paths. Map same-zone network communication, inter-zone communication and communication with untrusted networks.
  9. Define conduits and boundary controls. Assign conduit IDs and identify the boundary device or other control mechanism.
  10. Validate isolation and primary functionality. Resolve dependencies that prevent a zone from operating its primary function when isolated.
  11. Reconcile all UR E26 deliverables. Verify alignment with the Asset Inventory, CSDD, exclusion decisions, compensating measures and test procedure.
  12. Submit and resolve Society comments. Control comments, responses, decisions and affected-document updates.
  13. Maintain the ZCD during construction. Assess substitutions, interface changes, network changes, late equipment and construction deviations.
  14. Complete as-built verification. Confirm physical installation, segmentation, zone allocation, boundary devices and documented communication paths.
  15. Hand over the lifecycle baseline. Transfer the approved ZCD package, change history and controlled supporting records to the Shipowner.

8.1 Management of TBD Information

Early design submissions may contain “TBD” information where data is genuinely unavailable. Each TBD should have an owner, required source, closure milestone and identified impact on other documents. A TBD should not remain unresolved at commissioning where it affects scope, zone allocation, segmentation, boundary-device selection, untrusted-network connectivity, permitted communication, isolation capability, compensating measures or test coverage.

8.2 Typical Change Triggers

  • equipment-model substitution;
  • firmware, operating-system or application-version change;
  • addition or removal of an interface;
  • new VLAN, subnet, route or trunk;
  • firewall or ACL change;
  • new remote-access function;
  • new wireless connection;
  • equipment relocation;
  • new inter-zone communication;
  • new untrusted-network connection;
  • change to Supplier security capability;
  • change to exclusion or compensating-measure status.

9.2 Commissioning Verification

Before final commissioning, UR E26 §5.2 requires the Systems Integrator to submit the updated as-built design documents and test procedure, and carry out testing witnessed by the Society under the approved procedure.

During commissioning, the Systems Integrator must demonstrate that the onboard security zones match the approved ZCD, CSDD, Vessel Asset Inventory and relevant Supplier documents, and that security-zone boundaries permit only the traffic documented in the approved design. UR E26 gives physical inspection, network scanning, firewall-rule evaluation and port scanning as examples of suitable verification methods.

Regulatory reference: IACS UR E26 Rev.1, §4.2.1.4.3.

The following are evidence methods, not a complete list of required tests. The approved procedure must also address applicable network-protection and isolation demonstrations under §§4.2.2 and 4.4.3. Scanning is an example method under §4.2.1.4.3, not a universally required ZCD test. Any permitted omission of previously certified tests is decided by the Society under §5.2.1. Plan intrusive tests with the affected CBS suppliers and within the vessel’s safe test conditions.

Depending on the vessel and approved test plan, commissioning evidence may include:

  • inspection of equipment and cable installation;
  • switch, router and firewall configuration review;
  • VLAN and routing verification;
  • network and port scans;
  • rule-set comparison;
  • communication-path tests;
  • zone-isolation tests;
  • configuration exports and backups;
  • photographic evidence;
  • discrepancy and closure records.

Lifecycle-ready handover

The Systems Integrator hands over the documentation required by UR E26 §5. The Shipowner carries the operation-phase duties; continued CRSI support depends on its appointment. Recommended operation-phase evidence includes the current ZCD revision, change log, MoC records, boundary-device configuration reviews, firewall or ACL review records, network or port-scan results, isolation-test records, configuration backups and relevant Society correspondence.

10.3 IEC 62443

IEC 62443-3-2:2020 provides the supporting industrial-control-system methodology for partitioning a system under consideration into zones and conduits, assessing risk and documenting target security levels. IEC 62443-3-3:2013 provides detailed system security requirements used with defined zones and conduits. These standards are supporting references; the governing marine compliance basis remains IACS UR E26, the applicable Class Rules and Society decisions.

Class-specific verification

IACS UR E26 provides a common minimum baseline. Within their implementation of that baseline, Classification Societies may differ in document codes, accepted formats, treatment of inter-zone non-IP communication, evidence expected for segmentation, acceptance of equivalent boundary devices and commissioning-test depth. Interpretations affecting architecture, scope or acceptance should be agreed with the vessel’s Society.

11. Practical Review Appendices

Common ZCD Deficiencies
  1. Using departments or rooms as zones without a security-policy basis.
  2. Treating a VLAN identifier as proof of effective segmentation.
  3. Claiming physical segmentation while sharing the same Layer 2 switch.
  4. Omitting managed switches, firewalls, routers, gateways or remote-access devices.
  5. Omitting Owner-supplied or late-installed equipment.
  6. Showing only the public Internet as untrusted while omitting other out-of-scope networks.
  7. Omitting serial or discrete communication with untrusted systems.
  8. Showing a firewall symbol without identifying the connected zones, conduit or controlled-traffic reference.
  9. Combining navigation/communication with machinery/cargo contrary to UR E26.
  10. Failing to allocate wireless devices to a dedicated zone.
  11. Omitting the separate-zone requirement for networked local/backup controls covered by §4.4.2.
  12. Failing to confirm that each zone can be isolated without loss of primary functionality.
  13. Using inconsistent asset, zone and conduit identifiers across documents.
  14. Overloading the ZCD with sensitive addresses, ports and rule details that belong in controlled records.
  15. Using Purdue levels as the compliance basis instead of UR E26 zone requirements.
  16. Failing to update the design-stage drawing to the installed and tested as-built condition.

12. Conclusion

The Zones and Conduit Diagram is the central architectural document for vessel-level cyber resilience under IACS UR E26. It establishes the applicable security zones, CBS allocation, physical implementation, boundary-crossing communication, untrusted-network interfaces and the traceability linking Supplier information, ship-level design and verification evidence.

A compliant ZCD begins with accurate scope identification and Supplier data. It requires defensible zone-allocation criteria, correct physical or logical segmentation, complete interface analysis and controlled conduit definition. It must remain consistent with the Vessel Asset Inventory, CSDD, exclusion assessments, compensating countermeasures and Ship Cyber Resilience Test Procedure.

The quality of the final ZCD should be assessed against four questions:

  1. Does it represent the correct scope?
  2. Does it describe a defensible security architecture?
  3. Does it match the installed and tested vessel?
  4. Can the Shipowner maintain and use it throughout the operational survey cycle?

A ZCD that satisfies all four questions is more than a plan-approval drawing. It is the controlled architectural baseline for vessel cyber resilience.
