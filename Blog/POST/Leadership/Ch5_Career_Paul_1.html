Leadership and followership do not operate independently. This chapter is, before anything else, a self-examination of the follower I have been.

Prologue — The Followership I Had Forgotten

"Most people spend far more of their careers as followers than as leaders. The discipline of being a good follower is at least as demanding — and as consequential — as the discipline of being a good leader."

Robert Kelley's research at Carnegie Mellon, published in The Power of Followership (1992), produced a striking finding: in most organizations, leaders account for approximately 20% of outcomes. The remaining 80% is the aggregate contribution of followers. And yet the vast majority of leadership literature — and the vast majority of professional self-development effort — focuses on the 20%.

As I began leading teams, I found myself thinking increasingly about what made the best people around me effective. And I kept arriving at the same uncomfortable realization: I had spent years studying how to lead and very little time thinking about how to follow. The neglect was not theoretical — it had concrete, retrospective consequences I could identify by name.

That realization led me back to a text that contains some of the most concentrated behavioral case studies available: Romance of the Three Kingdoms, the 14th-century Chinese epic that chronicles three decades of political and military conflict among rival states. The text is often read as a study in strategic leadership — Zhuge Liang's planning, Cao Cao's decisiveness, Liu Bei's vision. But read differently, it is equally a study of how talented people destroy their own careers through four recurring failures of followership.

Taboo 1 — Xu You (許攸)
Do Not Leverage Personal Relationships Over Professional Hierarchy

Xu You had been close friends with Cao Cao since childhood. He had also provided critical intelligence that helped Cao Cao win the Battle of Guandu — arguably one of the decisive moments of the Three Kingdoms period. He was not without genuine value to the organization. But he persistently addressed Cao Cao by his childhood nickname in public — "A-Man" — and used their shared history to position himself above the institutional structure. He treated personal loyalty as a trump card that exempted him from professional protocol.

He was eventually executed by Xu Chu, one of Cao Cao's generals, after one public provocation too many. Cao Cao punished Xu Chu for the act — but made no serious effort to bring Xu You back.

Organizational hierarchy exists not to declare the worth of individuals but to define the structure of roles and responsibilities. When a personal relationship is used to bypass that structure — to claim access, to skip consultation, to speak more candidly than the role permits — it does not strengthen the relationship. It corrodes the trust of everyone else in the organization who is observing the transaction. Robert Greene, in The 48 Laws of Power (1998), identifies this pattern precisely: "Never assume that the relationship that helped you gain power will help you keep it." The context that made the relationship valuable is frequently different from the context in which you are now operating.

📌 In Maritime Organizations

In complex multi-party maritime projects — where shipowners, classification societies, shipbuilders, and equipment suppliers interact under tight contractual and regulatory frameworks — invoking a personal relationship as a shortcut to decision-making undermines the credibility of the entire team. The quality of the work is what sustains the relationship over time, not the relationship that was established before the work began. Familiarity without professional discipline is a liability in technical environments.

Taboo 2 — Kong Rong (孔融)
Do Not Complain Without Solutions

Kong Rong was a brilliant literary figure and a descendant of Confucius, which gave him substantial social prestige in the Han court structure. He was also, by most historical accounts, chronically dissatisfied — not as a driver of improvement, but as a performance of his own moral superiority. When Cao Cao introduced grain rationing and prohibited alcohol, Kong Rong publicly mocked the policy with a sarcastic reductio: if alcohol is forbidden because it causes disorder, should women not be forbidden as well? The wit was sharp. The contribution to the problem was zero.

He was eventually charged with sedition and executed. What destroyed him was not one remark but a consistent pattern of substituting intellectual performance for engaged problem-solving. He critiqued from a distance. He never claimed responsibility for outcomes.

Criticism inside organizations is not inherently destructive — it is often necessary. What determines whether it functions as leadership or as spectatorship is whether it is accompanied by any accountability. The person who says "this isn't working" and then waits to be proven right has made themselves a spectator of the problem. The person who says "this isn't working — here is what I think we should change, and I will take responsibility for testing it" has become a participant. Organizations can tolerate and even reward the second posture. The first, sustained over time, produces only friction.

Robert Kelley's followership research identified what he called "alienated followers" — individuals who are critical and independent in their thinking but passive in their engagement. They are often among the most intellectually capable people in the organization. They are also among the most organizationally costly, because their capability never converts into shared outcomes.

📌 In Maritime Organizations

When IMO regulations are revised or IACS Unified Requirements are strengthened, the response that distinguishes career-building professionals from critics is simple: What are we going to do about it? The professional who brings a compliance map, a gap analysis, and a proposed timeline to the next meeting will always outrun the one who spent the same time articulating why the regulation is unreasonable.

Taboo 3 — Yang Xiu (楊修)
Do Not Act as a Free Agent Inside an Organization

Yang Xiu was perhaps the most intellectually gifted person in Cao Cao's inner circle. He routinely anticipated Cao Cao's intentions before they were expressed — interpreting cryptic commands, solving coded riddles, predicting decisions. The historical records show him doing this correctly, repeatedly. His pattern recognition was exceptional.

What ended him was not a single act of insubordination but a cumulative pattern of operating ahead of authorization. During the Hanzhong campaign, he preemptively ordered preparations for withdrawal based on his interpretation of Cao Cao's mood — without being directed to. He was correct about the likely outcome. He was wrong about his authorization to act on that prediction. Cao Cao had him executed for "disturbing the heart of the army."

In any structured organization, individual talent becomes an asset only when it operates within sanctioned boundaries. The moment a talented individual begins to act on their own judgment without explicit authorization — adjusting scope, making commitments to external parties, or making decisions that belong to a superior — they are not leading. They are appropriating authority they have not been granted. Sun Tzu's principle in The Art of War is directly relevant: "The general who advances without coveting fame and retreats without fearing disgrace, whose only thought is to protect his country and do good service for his sovereign, is the jewel of the kingdom." The operative structure is "service for his sovereign" — not "acting on his own best judgment."

Talent aligned with the organization's direction is compounding. Talent that runs ahead of it creates organizational anxiety, political friction, and eventually, removal — not because the talent is wrong but because the trust infrastructure required to support autonomous action has not been built.

📌 In Maritime Organizations

In maritime cybersecurity and digital transformation projects, this pattern appears when a technically strong team member adjusts scope with the client without informing the project leader, or makes architectural decisions that belong at a higher authorization level. The decision may be technically correct. The absence of coordination undermines the team's collective credibility. Capability without direction is not power — it is turbulence.

Taboo 4 — Cui Yan (崔琰)
Do Not Resist the Direction After the Decision Is Made

Cui Yan was one of the most respected officials in Cao Cao's administration. His reputation for integrity was genuine and widely acknowledged. He consistently held principled positions — including positions that Cao Cao ultimately did not adopt. That was not his error. His error was continuing to signal opposition after decisions had been finalized, and allowing that opposition to become visible enough to create factionalism.

When a letter he wrote was construed as expressing contempt for Cao Cao's succession plans, he was imprisoned and eventually forced to suicide. The ambiguity of his final letter — whether it was sarcasm or genuine expression — may never be resolved. What is clear is that he had, by that point, accumulated enough visible opposition that the interpretation of ambiguity ran against him.

There is a clear and important distinction between input before a decision and resistance after a decision. The first is a professional responsibility — failing to provide honest counsel when it is invited is a failure of followership. The second is organizational dysfunction. Once a decision has been finalized and execution has begun, continuing to publicly question it or to signal disagreement to colleagues is not principled integrity — it is a disruption to the coordinated effort the decision was intended to produce.

"If the bus has already departed and you believe the route is wrong — you should exit. What you cannot do is demand that the driver change routes while the bus is in motion."

📌 In Maritime Organizations

In project environments, the post-decision phase is where alignment either holds or fractures. A team member who continues to reference a rejected approach — in client meetings, in internal briefings, in informal conversations — introduces doubt that compounds across stakeholders. If the decision was wrong, the correct response is to document the concern, track the outcome, and raise it in the retrospective when the evidence is available. The correct response is not to relitigate it through parallel channels while execution is underway. If you want to influence the next decision, be present and committed for this one.

The Pattern Behind the Pattern
What These Four Figures Share

Xu You, Kong Rong, Yang Xiu, and Cui Yan were not ordinary people. They were, by the standards of their era, exceptional — intellectually capable, socially connected, politically experienced. They did not fail because they lacked talent. They failed because, in different ways, they each treated the organizational context as something that existed to accommodate them, rather than as a structure they were responsible for sustaining.

Four Failure Modes of Exceptional Followers
① Treating the relationship as an asset to draw down on (Xu You)

Relationships are built through consistent professional performance, not through the exercise of prior personal history. Drawing on history in place of performance depletes the relationship and signals to others that you have stopped earning your place.

② Substituting critique for contribution (Kong Rong)

The gap between "identifying what's wrong" and "fixing what's wrong" is where careers are built or abandoned. One requires observation. The other requires commitment to an outcome — and the willingness to be held responsible for it.

③ Acting on individual judgment ahead of collective authorization (Yang Xiu)

Autonomous action requires autonomous trust, and autonomous trust is granted, not claimed. The follower who moves ahead of authorization may be correct in their assessment and still be wrong in their action.

④ Continuing to fight the decision after execution has begun (Cui Yan)

Post-decision resistance is not principled integrity. It is a failure to commit, and it has consequences not only for the individual but for everyone who is trying to execute in good faith.

Warren Bennis, in On Becoming a Leader (1989), made an observation that has stayed with me: "The most dangerous leadership myth is that leaders are born — that there is a genetic factor to leadership. This myth asserts that people simply either have certain charismatic qualities or not. That's nonsense; in fact, the opposite is true. Leaders are made rather than born." The same is true of followership. The capacity to be a trustworthy, productive, and aligned member of a team is not a natural disposition — it is a discipline that is chosen, practiced, and refined. And it begins with an honest inventory of which of these four taboos you recognize in yourself.

Epilogue — What I See When I Look Back

Looking back across more than eighteen years in maritime industries — across shipbuilders, shipping companies, classification societies, and consulting engagements — I can identify each of these four failures in my own past with uncomfortable specificity. I have, at various points, invoked relationships when I should have let performance speak. I have critiqued without committing. I have moved ahead of my authorization. I have relitigated decisions that were already made.

None of these failures happened because I didn't know better in the abstract. They happened because in the moment, each one felt justified — even virtuous. That is precisely what makes them taboos rather than obvious mistakes. Obvious mistakes are easy to avoid. The taboos in this chapter feel, from the inside, like integrity, engagement, initiative, and principle. The test is not how they feel from the inside but what they produce from the outside — in the organization, in the team, in the relationship.

Leadership requires excellent followers. And excellent followership is not passive — it is the most demanding form of organizational discipline. Knowing when to lead, when to follow, and when to simply commit and execute is the full literacy of organizational life. Most of us arrive at that literacy late, and often only through the cost of its absence.

🧭 Captain Paul's Take

"The lesson I keep returning to is not that these four men were wrong in their judgments. They were often right. The lesson is that being right is not enough. Organizational life is not a truth competition — it is a coordinated effort toward shared outcomes. The follower who is right but whose rightness fractures the team is not an asset. The follower who is occasionally wrong but whose commitment keeps the team coherent is. Followership, done well, is a form of leadership in itself."

Xu You, Kong Rong, Yang Xiu, Cui Yan — they were not villains. They were mirrors. The discipline is in looking honestly at what they reflect.
