Prologue

For years I have thought about writing a book drawn from my professional experience. Early on, the book I imagined was about technology — AI, cybersecurity, digital transformation, the restructuring of maritime industries. That seemed like the territory I knew best.

But as the years accumulated, my attention shifted — gradually and then decisively — toward people and organizations. The hardest problems I encountered were never technical. They were human. Strategy without alignment fails. Capability without trust disappears. And the most talented people I knew often ended up at the edges of the organizations that needed them most.

That shift is what produced this series. Its title is "A Leader's Crisis and the Psychology of Growth." It begins with a question that sounds simple but carries enormous weight.

"Why do the most capable people in an organization so often end up disappearing from it?"

Part 1

The Paradox of Competence — Why Yi Sun-sin Went to Prison

In 1597, Korea's greatest naval commander was imprisoned. His record was unmatched: 23 battles, 23 victories, zero defeats. Through tactical innovations like the Crane Wing Formation and the iron-plated Turtle Ship, Admiral Yi Sun-sin had severed Japanese supply lines, preserved Korea's western sea lanes, and effectively saved the kingdom from complete collapse. His capability was, by any rational measure, irreplaceable.

And yet it was precisely this irreplaceability that led to his downfall.

The official charge was defying a royal command. But the real forces at work were organizational and psychological. When a single individual's performance comes to define the survival of an entire institution, everyone else — especially those above — begins to feel diminished. The system, almost reflexively, moves to neutralize the threat.

13

Despite this record, he was imprisoned in 1597, stripped of rank, and forced to serve as a common soldier. He was restored to command only when the navy faced total collapse without him.

This is not simply a historical curiosity. Jeffrey Pfeffer, in his landmark study Power: Why Some People Have It and Others Don't (2010), documented how organizations systematically work against those whose competence most threatens the existing hierarchy. The mechanism is not always conscious. It manifests as subtle exclusion, attribution of achievement to others, amplification of minor errors, and restriction of access to information.

The data confirms the pattern. Analysis of organizational turnover consistently shows that top performers — the top 5% by measured contribution — leave at rates disproportionate to their performance. The primary cited cause is not compensation, not career ceilings, and not workload. It is organizational politics: the sense that excellence is not rewarded, and may in fact be penalized.

💡 Modern Yi Sun-sins

Steve Jobs was removed from Apple — the company he founded — in 1985. His capabilities were undeniable; the organizational friction he generated was equally undeniable. The Peter Principle, articulated by Laurence J. Peter and Raymond Hull in 1969, notes that organizations promote people until they reach their level of incompetence. Its lesser-known corollary: organizations sometimes remove people who have already exceeded what the hierarchy can comfortably accommodate.

Part 2

Why Organizations Fear Their Best People — Power Threat Theory

Organizational psychology gives us a framework for understanding what happened to Yi Sun-sin: Power Threat Theory. When an individual's capability grows to a point where it structurally challenges the existing power hierarchy, the organization activates a kind of immune response. It is not a conspiracy — it is an emergent system behavior, as natural as any organizational self-preservation mechanism.

The pattern is remarkably consistent across industries, cultures, and centuries. What does it look like in practice?

What the organization does

  • Attributes individual achievements to the team
  • Amplifies minor mistakes disproportionately
  • Gradually restricts access to information
  • Engineers informal isolation within the team
  • Creates structural obstacles to visibility

What the high performer experiences

  • "I don't know what I did wrong"
  • "The harder I work, the harder it gets"
  • "My colleagues have distanced themselves"
  • "Results don't translate to recognition"
  • "I feel like a threat, not an asset"

Amy Edmondson, in The Fearless Organization (2018), identifies psychological safety as the critical variable in high-performance teams. When an individual's competence most threatens the existing hierarchy, that psychological safety is precisely what gets withdrawn. The environment becomes one where speaking up, proposing change, or simply outperforming the norm carries structural risk.

In maritime and shipbuilding industries, I have watched this pattern repeat itself with uncomfortable regularity. An engineer whose technical mastery drives a critical innovation gets promoted to management — and then gradually marginalized as a "technical outsider" who doesn't understand commercial priorities. A cybersecurity lead who builds proactive defenses ahead of the regulatory curve becomes a budget reduction target the moment the organization needs to demonstrate cost discipline. The competence is real. The structural rejection is equally real.

"There is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success, than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things."

— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince (1513)

Machiavelli was writing about political innovation in Renaissance Italy. But his observation maps precisely onto the experience of organizational high performers. Disruption — even beneficial, necessary disruption — generates resistance proportional to the threat it poses to the established order. The more indispensable the disruptor, the more urgent the resistance.

This is the first core question of this leadership series. The organizational dynamic is not going away. The question for any high performer is not whether this pattern exists, but what to do when you find yourself inside it.

Part 3

If You Are Yi Sun-sin — Three Paths Forward

After long reflection on this question — in my own career and in the careers of people I have worked alongside — I see three viable paths for a high performer caught inside this structural dynamic. None is universally correct. Each carries a real cost. But naming them clearly is the first step toward making a conscious choice rather than simply absorbing the consequence.

Path A  |  The Dimming Strategy

Deliberately channel individual achievement into collective credit. Step back from individual visibility. Manage the perceived threat to the hierarchy. Organizational survival probability increases — but the cost is never fully deploying your capabilities. Research on self-monitoring in high-performance contexts (Snyder, 1987) identifies what follows: a slow erosion of authentic engagement, eventually producing the burnout not of overwork but of chronic self-suppression.

Cost: Internal depletion — burning out from within, not from overload

Path B  |  Change from Within

Work systematically to reshape organizational culture from the inside. Build trust infrastructure over years. Demonstrate that change serves the organization's own long-term interest. If successful, the entire organization transforms — a multiplier effect that no external change can match. Liz Wiseman, in Multipliers (2010), documents how leaders who amplify others' intelligence can shift organizational cultures at scale. But the risk is proportionally severe: failure here often accelerates the very exit it was designed to prevent.

Cost: High risk, long time horizon, requires deep institutional patience

Path C  |  Build Your Own Table

Leave, and build. Found the organization — or find the role — where your method and your values define the rules of the game. Yi Sun-sin, after imprisonment and white-coat service, returned to command 13 ships at Myeongnyang against 133. He could not fight on the court that had rejected him. So he fought on his own terms. The initial cost is real: uncertainty, isolation, the absence of established infrastructure. But on your own stage, the structural dynamic reverses. You are no longer the threat. You are the architect.

Cost: Uncertainty, resource scarcity in early stages, psychological isolation

No external observer can tell you which path is right. Your circumstances, your risk tolerance, your commitments, and your values are not visible from outside. But one thing is clear across all three paths.

"Yi Sun-sin was great not because he won at sea. He was great because he did not break in prison, did not stop during white-coat service, and did not retreat at Myeongnyang. The resilience — not the victories — is what defines him."

⚓ Captain Paul's Take

When I began writing this piece, I realized I was writing about myself. In maritime cybersecurity, the paradox of competence is not abstract — it is operational. The more precisely your technical capabilities differentiate you, the more carefully the surrounding hierarchy watches. I have felt both simultaneously: welcomed for results, scrutinized for status implications.

I chose Path C — not because it was the easiest, but because it was the most honest. I built my own stage. This series exists because I believe these experiences deserve clear-eyed examination, not complaint or resentment, but clarity: about what actually happens inside organizations, and about the choices available to those navigating it.

The next chapter asks a harder question: if the organization won't lead you the way you need, who will? The answer may surprise you.
