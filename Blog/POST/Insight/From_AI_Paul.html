In the AI Era, the Next Competition for Smart Ships Is Not 'Performance' but 'Trust'

Over the next five years, the real change in the maritime industry won't come from AI model advancement — it will come from the evolution of international regulations and cybersecurity frameworks built around AI

AI Agent · Smart Ship Maritime Cybersecurity IMO · IACS · IEC Cyber Resilience

"Over the next five years, the biggest change in the maritime industry will not be the advancement of AI itself,
but the evolution of international regulations and cybersecurity frameworks built on the assumption of AI."

This statement might seem somewhat unexpected. Many people assume that competition in the AI industry will move toward developing ever more capable models — believing that as large language models like GPT, Claude, Gemini, and Llama keep advancing, that progress alone will define industry competitiveness. But from the perspective of smart ships, the story is a little different.

A ship is not an ordinary IT service. It is a Safety-Critical System responsible for human lives, the marine environment, and cargo worth billions of dollars. In this environment, the "most trustworthy AI" matters far more than the "smartest AI."

Ⅰ. AI Is No Longer a 'User' of the Ship — It Is Becoming an 'Operator'

Until now, AI has been a tool that assists people — analyzing voyage records, drafting maintenance reports, or summarizing operational data. Going forward, however, the situation will change.

The Shifting Role of AI Agents — Tool → Digital Operator
BEFORE — AI as Tool
Assisting with voyage data analysis
Drafting maintenance reports
Summarizing and translating data
AFTER — AI as Digital Operator
Connecting to and reasoning across the Navigation System
Controlling Engine / Cargo systems
A digital operator that makes decisions on its own

AI Agents will take on the role of a Digital Operator — connecting diverse systems such as the Navigation System, Loading Computer, Engine Control System, and Cargo Management System, and making judgments on their own.

In other words, AI is no longer simple software — it becomes a member directly participating in the ship's operational process. And this raises an important question.

"How far can we trust AI's judgment?"

This question will become the most important topic in the smart ship industry going forward.

Ⅱ. The Always Connected Ship Is Both a New Opportunity and a New Risk

In the past, ships had only limited connectivity to the outside world. But today, with the spread of low-earth-orbit satellite communication technologies such as Starlink Maritime, ships are effectively becoming an Always Connected environment.

✅ Positive Changes
Real-time vessel status monitoring from shore
AI-based Predictive Maintenance
Digital-twin-based voyage optimization
⚠️ New Risks
Exposure of cloud and remote maintenance systems
AI Agent and software update servers
The entire supply chain expanding as an attack surface

"Always Connected Ship is Always Exposed Ship."

Going forward, competitiveness will be defined not by how much a ship is connected, but by how safely it is connected.

Ⅲ. In the AI Era, the Meaning of Cybersecurity Also Changes

Until now, maritime cybersecurity has centered on protecting the network. But once AI begins participating in operational decision-making, what needs to be protected changes as well.

🔍
Explainability
It must be possible to explain why AI arrived at a given decision. Black-box AI is difficult to accept in a Safety-Critical environment.
🛡️
Data Integrity
It must be possible to verify that input data has not been tampered with. AI can make incorrect judgments based on contaminated data.
📋
Auditability
It must be possible to trace who used AI and under what authority. This is an essential condition for establishing clear accountability.
🔄 Paradigm Shift

The purpose of cybersecurity is expanding from protecting systems to guaranteeing Trusted Decisions. This is the essence of maritime cybersecurity in the AI era.

Ⅳ. Ultimately, International Regulations Will Also Shift to Center on AI

Today, maritime cybersecurity is developing around regulations such as IMO MSC.428(98), IACS UR E26/E27, and IEC 62443. All of these regulations emphasize Cyber Resilience. However, most of them were written on the assumption that ships are operated by people.

Future regulations will not simply protect systems — they will evolve into regulations that make AI trustworthy. This is the new regulatory territory that IMO, IACS, IEC, and ISO will inevitably have to address.

Ⅵ. The Regulatory Framework Is Catching Up — Faster Than Most Expect

For years, maritime AI operated in a regulatory grey zone. Ship operators deployed machine learning tools for route optimization and predictive maintenance without any binding framework governing how those systems should behave, fail, or be audited. That grey zone is closing.

IMO's Maritime Safety Committee has begun embedding AI-related considerations into existing cybersecurity frameworks. MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3, originally designed for cyber risk management, is now being reinterpreted by flag states to include AI-driven decision systems. Meanwhile, IACS Unified Requirements E26 and E27, which came into force in July 2024, establish connectivity and cyber resilience requirements that directly affect how AI agents can interface with onboard OT systems.

"AI on ships must be explainable, auditable, and fail-safe."

It is not enough for an AI agent to make the right decision most of the time. It must be able to demonstrate — to a port state control officer, a classification surveyor, or an accident investigator — how and why it made each decision.

IEC 62443, the industrial cybersecurity standard being adapted for maritime OT environments, adds another layer. It demands that any system with network connectivity — including AI inference engines — be segmented, monitored, and hardened against adversarial manipulation. In an environment where a falsified AIS position or a spoofed sensor reading could lead an AI navigator toward a collision, data integrity is not a feature. It is a precondition for safe operation.

Ⅶ. What Shipowners and Operators Should Be Doing Now

The gap between AI capability and AI trustworthiness on ships is not just a technology problem — it is a governance problem. Shipowners who treat AI adoption as a software procurement exercise will find themselves exposed. Those who build trust frameworks around their AI systems will be better positioned for the regulatory environment ahead. Practically, this means three things.

🗂️
Document the decision boundary
Define which decisions AI may make autonomously, which require human confirmation, and which are off-limits. This boundary should be written into the Safety Management System.
🎯
Treat AI inputs as attack surfaces
Sensor data, AIS feeds, voyage plan inputs — every data stream that feeds an AI agent is a potential vector for manipulation. OT security must extend upstream to protect the integrity of AI inputs, not just the outputs.
📋
Prepare for the audit
Classification societies are developing AI-specific notation schemes. DNV, Lloyd's Register, and Bureau Veritas have all published frameworks for verifying AI systems onboard. Early engagement with your class society will save significant remediation cost later.

The maritime industry has always adapted to new technology through regulation — from SOLAS to MARPOL to the ISM Code. AI will be no different. The question is not whether rules will come, but whether your organization will be ready when they do.

The next five years will be less about competing on AI model performance, and more about how trustworthy we can make AI. And at the center of that effort stand Smart Ships, Maritime OT, Cyber Resilience, and international regulation.
