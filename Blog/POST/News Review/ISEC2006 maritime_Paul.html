💡 Insight 📋 Event Review ISEC 2026 CYTUR

Inside the Maritime Cybersecurity Conference 2026 — And a Moment I Didn't See Coming on Hanwha Ocean's Slide

Attendee Review · CYTUR × ISEC 2026, Seoul — August 12, 2026

Captain Paul
Maritime 4.0 · AI & Cyber Intelligence · August 2026

On August 12, 2026, I sat in the Asem Ballroom at COEX, Seoul, for the Maritime Cybersecurity Conference 2026, organized by CYTUR (싸이터) and co-hosted alongside ISEC 2026 — the 20th International Security Conference, one of Asia's largest integrated cybersecurity events, running concurrently across COEX Hall D, the Auditorium, and the Asem Ballroom.

Seven speakers from across Korea's maritime and cybersecurity sectors took the stage — regulators, integrators, shipbuilders, and vendors — covering everything from GNSS-era regulatory change to naval-vessel cyber capability. I went in expecting a solid regulatory-update session. I did not expect to see my own work referenced from the podium.

Here is what I heard, why it mattered, and the moment that made this one worth writing up properly.

Ⅰ. The Lineup — What I Went There to Hear

Seven sessions, each from a different vantage point on the same problem — how does an industry that has spent a century optimizing for steel and seamanship now engineer for cyber resilience:

  • CYTUR CEO Cho Yong-hyeon — Maritime Cybersecurity Regulations & Technology Trend Changes
  • CYTUR CTO Yoon Young-hwan — How Cyber Resilience Is Implemented on Ships
  • Hanwha Ocean Team Lead — New Challenges and Responses for Ships in the AI Era
  • SeaNet Team Lead — The Role and Readiness of Maritime SI Firms in Cybersecurity
  • KEYPAIR CEO Lee Chang-geun — Responding to AI-Based Attacks and the Quantum Computing Era
  • RINA Team Lead Choi Gyu-oh — Cybersecurity Framework for Autonomous Ships
  • HD HHI EVP Jeon Chang-su — Cybersecurity Capabilities of Naval Vessels
What struck me sitting through all seven back to back was how little overlap there was. A regulator, two toolmakers, a shipyard, an SI integrator, a class society, and a defense-side yard — each treating cyber resilience as a different engineering problem, not a shared checkbox.

Ⅱ. The Backdrop — Why the Room Was Full

The numbers behind the agenda explain the turnout. According to CYTUR's 2026 Maritime Cyber Threat White Paper, released ahead of the conference, maritime cyber incidents surged 103 percent year-on-year.

A year where incidents more than double is not a background statistic to open a keynote with. It is the reason seven senior people from seven different organizations agreed to share a stage on the same afternoon.

📌 The Moment I Wasn't Expecting

ShipPaulJobs Referenced — Hanwha Ocean Session

During the Hanwha Ocean session, "New Challenges and Responses for Ships in the AI Era," I saw ShipPaulJobs appear in the presenter's reference list. This was not something I arranged or was told about in advance — I found out sitting in the audience, watching the slide.

It was unsolicited. No one asked me to prepare a quote, and no one told me it was coming. It was simply a senior professional at one of Korea's largest shipbuilders deciding, on their own, that something I had written was worth putting in front of that room.

Being listed in the References of an academic paper or a technical presentation means your work has been recognized as a credible source of information. It's not the same as an official corporate sponsorship — and I'd argue that's exactly what makes it feel more genuine. No one paid for that slot. It was a natural, academic-style acknowledgment, made because the content held up on its own.

I want to be careful not to overstate this — one citation on one slide is one data point, not a trend. But it is the kind of data point that tells you independent, practitioner-written analysis is reaching the people actually building these ships, not just other writers.

Ⅲ. Why This Matters Beyond the Conference Room

None of the frameworks referenced on stage that day are theoretical anymore. IACS UR E26/E27, IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3, and the newly-entered-into-force MASS Code were all discussed as live, operational constraints — not future guidance to prepare for eventually.

ShipPaulJobs exists to sit exactly in that gap — between the regulatory text and what a shipyard, integrator, or supplier actually has to build. When a practitioner cites independent work like this in front of industry peers, it is a small but real signal that the gap is being bridged from both directions.

Seven speakers walked in with seven different problems.

They walked out agreeing on one: the threat curve has already bent upward.

And somewhere in that room, a slide I never expected to see reminded me why I keep writing this blog.

Captain's Take

Events like ISEC 2026 matter because they put regulators, shipyards, vendors, and class societies in the same room, on the same day — forced to talk about the same 103% number instead of their own siloed version of it.

Seeing ShipPaulJobs referenced from that stage was not the reason I attended — but it is the reason I am writing this review instead of just a set of notes. If your organization is navigating IACS UR E26/E27, IMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3, or MASS Code implementation, I'd welcome the conversation.
