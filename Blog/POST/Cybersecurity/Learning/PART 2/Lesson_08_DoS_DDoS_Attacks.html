DoS and DDoS Attacks

Denial of Service · Distributed DoS · Volumetric · Protocol · Application-layer attacks · Botnets · Amplification · Scrubbing · Maritime port and communication system risk — attacks targeting Availability, the third pillar of the CIA Triad.

Captain Paul
Maritime Cybersecurity · IACS UR E26/E27

🎯 Lesson Objective

After this lesson you can distinguish DoS from DDoS attacks, classify DDoS attack types (volumetric, protocol, application-layer), explain how botnets are used to generate attack traffic and how amplification attacks multiply that traffic, describe the maritime risk areas most exposed to availability attacks, and outline the primary DDoS mitigation approaches including CDN offloading and scrubbing centre services.

In October 2021, the Port of Houston in Texas was targeted by a cyberattack that caused disruption to the port authority's IT systems. While attribution and the full extent of the attack were not fully disclosed publicly, it highlighted what security professionals had long warned: port authorities and maritime logistics systems are increasingly targets for attacks designed to cause operational disruption rather than data theft.

Among the most straightforward forms of such disruption is the Denial of Service attack. Unlike ransomware or data theft — which require sophisticated persistence and lateral movement — a DoS or DDoS attack has one simple objective: make a system, service, or network unavailable to its legitimate users. It targets Availability — the A in the CIA Triad.

For maritime operations, availability is not an abstract security concern. When a port's vessel traffic management system (VTMS) is unavailable, berth scheduling fails. When a ship's satellite communication system is overwhelmed, crew welfare, voyage management, and emergency communication all degrade simultaneously. When a shipping company's freight booking portal goes offline during a peak shipping window, the operational and financial impact is immediate.

This lesson examines how DoS and DDoS attacks work technically, why DDoS is difficult to defend against using perimeter controls alone, and what mitigation strategies exist.

1. DoS vs DDoS — What's the Difference?

A Denial of Service (DoS) attack originates from a single source — one attacker, one machine, one connection. The attacker sends traffic or requests at a volume or in a format that the target system cannot handle, causing it to slow down, crash, or become unreachable. Early DoS attacks were simple: flood a server with more packets than it can process. Single-source DoS is now relatively easy to defend against — block the source IP and the attack stops.

A Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack coordinates traffic from thousands or millions of sources simultaneously. Because the traffic comes from diverse IP addresses across multiple countries and ISPs, blocking individual sources is impractical. The aggregated bandwidth of the attack can exceed what the target organisation's upstream internet connection can absorb — meaning the attack is effective even before it reaches the target server.

Three Categories of DDoS Attack
Volumetric

Overwhelm the target's bandwidth with raw traffic volume. Measured in Gbps or Tbps. Examples: UDP Flood, ICMP Flood, DNS Amplification, NTP Amplification.

Scale: Largest attacks have exceeded 3 Tbps. A 1 Gbps internet connection is overwhelmed by a modest volumetric attack.
Protocol

Exhaust network infrastructure resources (firewalls, load balancers, routers) by exploiting weaknesses in layer 3/4 protocol behaviour. Examples: SYN Flood, Ping of Death, Smurf Attack.

SYN Flood: Sends thousands of TCP SYN requests without completing the handshake, filling the server's connection table until it cannot accept new connections.
Application Layer

Targets web application resources at layer 7 (HTTP/HTTPS). Mimics legitimate user traffic so it is harder to filter. Examples: HTTP Flood (GET/POST), Slowloris, RUDY, DNS Query Flood.

Slowloris: Opens many HTTP connections and keeps them alive with partial requests, exhausting the server's connection pool without high bandwidth.

2. Botnets and Amplification — How DDoS Scales

The power of DDoS comes from scale. A single attacker cannot generate terabits of traffic. DDoS achieves this through two mechanisms: botnets and amplification.

🤖 Botnets

A botnet is a network of compromised devices — PCs, servers, IoT cameras, routers, maritime satellite terminals — that have been infected with malware placing them under attacker control. The attacker (the "bot herder") commands all devices simultaneously to send traffic to the target.

The Mirai botnet (2016) demonstrated the IoT dimension: it compromised hundreds of thousands of internet-connected cameras and DVRs using default credentials, generating a 1.2 Tbps attack against Dyn DNS that took down Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, and many other major services.

Maritime risk: Vessel-connected IoT devices (CCTV, HVAC sensors, cargo monitoring) with default credentials can be recruited into botnets without the ship operator's knowledge.

📡 Amplification Attacks

Amplification exploits protocols where a small request generates a large response. The attacker spoofs the victim's IP address as the source and sends small requests to thousands of open servers — all of which send large responses to the victim.

DNS Amplification: A 60-byte DNS query can generate a 3,000-byte response — an amplification factor of 50x. NTP Amplification (using the monlist command) can reach amplification factors of 556x. The attacker generates 100 Mbps of outbound traffic but the victim receives 5.5 Gbps.

Defence: Network operators implement BCP38 (source address validation) to block spoofed traffic; DNS and NTP servers should be configured to prevent amplification.

3. Maritime Systems at Risk from Availability Attacks

Maritime Availability Attack Surface
Port Management Systems VTMS (Vessel Traffic Management), Terminal Operating Systems (TOS), and port community systems are internet-connected and handle time-critical berth scheduling, cargo handling, and customs documentation. Outage even for hours causes cascading delays across the supply chain.
Fleet Management Portals Web-based fleet management, voyage planning, and freight booking systems are externally accessible and targeted by attacks when competitors, state actors, or hacktivists wish to disrupt a shipping company's operations.
Satellite Communications VSAT and LEO satellite terminals can be overwhelmed or jammed at the radio layer. While jamming is not a cyber attack in the traditional sense, satellite service denial impacts vessel communications, crew welfare systems, and remote monitoring simultaneously.
AIS & GMDSS Systems AIS (Automatic Identification System) data feeds to third-party tracking sites and coast guard systems can be targeted. AIS jamming and spoofing are distinct from DDoS but share the outcome: degrading situational awareness for vessel traffic in a region.
Shipyard & Classification Systems Design systems, approval workflows, and certification databases — if unavailable — halt new construction projects and compliance processes. Ransomware groups have targeted shipyards specifically for this leverage.

4. DDoS Mitigation Strategies

DDoS mitigation cannot rely on perimeter firewalls alone — if the attack traffic exceeds the upstream bandwidth capacity, the connection is saturated before it reaches the firewall. Effective mitigation requires services positioned upstream of the target's internet connection.

☁️ CDN / Anycast DDoS Protection

Services like Cloudflare, Akamai, and AWS Shield use anycast routing to distribute attack traffic across globally distributed nodes. Each node absorbs a fraction of the total attack volume. The legitimate origin server is never directly exposed to the raw attack traffic.

🔄 Upstream Scrubbing Centres

ISP-level DDoS mitigation services redirect attack traffic to scrubbing centres — high-capacity filtering infrastructure that distinguishes malicious from legitimate traffic and forwards only clean traffic to the target. Requires BGP routing agreements with the ISP.

🚦 Rate Limiting & Traffic Shaping

For application-layer attacks that do not saturate bandwidth, rate limiting (restricting requests per IP, per session, per endpoint) can prevent server resource exhaustion. Web Application Firewalls (WAF) can identify and block HTTP flood patterns.

📋 Incident Response Plan

A pre-agreed DDoS response playbook — including contacts at the ISP, scrubbing provider, and internal escalation tree — reduces response time from hours to minutes. Maritime operators should include DDoS scenarios in their cyber incident response exercises.

✍️ Author Insight — From the Field
Captain Paul · Maritime Cybersecurity Consultant · IACS UR E26/E27

In E26 CRSI assessments, DDoS typically ranks lower than ransomware and phishing in the threat prioritisation discussions — partly because it does not result in data theft and partly because maritime operators see it as something that happens to internet companies, not shipping companies. This is a mistake that is becoming increasingly expensive.

The maritime sector's increasing digitisation — online freight booking, real-time vessel tracking, electronic documentation, web-based port clearance systems — has made availability a genuine operational dependency. A shipping company that cannot accept freight bookings for 12 hours during peak season because its portal is under DDoS attack suffers direct revenue loss, customer relationship damage, and potential regulatory reporting obligations under cyber incident reporting rules.

The simplest actionable step for maritime operators is to ensure that externally accessible web services are behind a CDN or DDoS mitigation provider. The cost of a Cloudflare or Akamai subscription is modest compared to the operational cost of even a short availability outage. This should be a baseline requirement for any internet-facing service in a maritime organisation's digital infrastructure.

✅ Key Takeaways — Lesson 8
  • DoS (single source) and DDoS (distributed, multiple sources) attacks target Availability — the A in CIA Triad.
  • Three DDoS categories: Volumetric (bandwidth flooding), Protocol (state table exhaustion), Application-layer (HTTP/HTTPS flood).
  • Botnets (infected IoT/PC networks) and amplification (exploiting UDP protocols like DNS/NTP to multiply traffic) are how DDoS achieves massive scale.
  • Maritime systems at risk include port VTS/TOS, fleet management portals, satellite comms, and AIS data feeds.
  • Perimeter firewalls cannot stop volumetric DDoS — upstream CDN, anycast distribution, and ISP scrubbing services are required.
  • DDoS incident response plans — with pre-agreed ISP/scrubbing contacts — must be prepared before an attack, not during one.
