As digital innovation accelerates across the maritime industry — from autonomous ships and smart ports to marine IoT — so does the attack surface available to adversaries. If security is not considered from the earliest stages of R&D, emerging technologies accumulate vulnerabilities that can lead to serious incidents at sea. Threat modeling is the structured practice of identifying, analyzing, and mitigating security threats during the design phase — before vulnerabilities are baked into a product.

Ⅰ. Why Is Threat Modeling Important in Maritime R&D?

Incorporating threat modeling into R&D is not only about strengthening security — it increases technological reliability and reduces long-term operational costs. Four core reasons drive its adoption in maritime technology development.

① Early Risk Elimination
Fixing vulnerabilities post-deployment is exponentially more costly. Security integrated at the design phase eliminates critical risks before they become incidents.
② New Attack Vectors
AI, IoT, cloud, and autonomous systems introduce threat landscapes that traditional security models cannot address. Threat modeling anticipates novel attack paths.
③ Regulatory Alignment
Maritime organizations must comply with IMO guidelines, NIST SP 800-82, and IEC 62443. Threat modeling provides the structured evidence required for regulatory approval.
④ Market Credibility
A security-first product earns shipowner and shipyard trust, providing a competitive advantage in vendor evaluations and procurement decisions.

Ⅱ. Threat Modeling Process in R&D (5 Steps)

Threat modeling is not a list of risks — it is a structured analytical process. The following five steps can be embedded into any maritime R&D workflow.

Step 1 — Define Critical Assets and System Scope

Identify the key assets that require protection and define the operational boundaries of the system under development. For autonomous ship R&D, critical assets include:

  • Navigation systems (AI-based route planning)
  • Satellite communication systems (VSAT, GPS)
  • Sensor networks (Radar, LiDAR, AIS)
  • Data servers and cloud platforms
Step 2 — Identify Potential Threats: The STRIDE Model

The STRIDE model classifies threats into six categories. Originally developed at Microsoft, it maps naturally to maritime OT/IT systems.

Letter Threat Type Description Maritime Example
S Spoofing Identity forgery GPS spoofing to alter an autonomous ship's route
T Tampering Data manipulation Hacking a port logistics system to modify cargo data
R Repudiation Denying an attack Hiding evidence of unauthorized remote access
I Info. Disclosure Data leakage Theft of shipowner and vessel data from cloud systems
D Denial of Service Disrupting services DDoS attacks shutting down ship communication systems
E Elevation of Privilege Unauthorized control Malware hijacking ship navigation systems
Step 3 — Analyze Attack Paths (Data Flow Diagrams)

Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs) visualize how data moves through a system and where adversaries can intercept it. A GPS spoofing attack on an autonomous ship follows this path:

Step 1 → Attacker injects malware into the satellite communication network
Step 2 → False GPS signals are broadcast to manipulate the ship's reported position
Step 3 → Navigation AI accepts spoofed coordinates as authentic
Result → Ship is misdirected into a dangerous or unauthorized area
Step 4 — Assess Risks: DREAD & CVSS Scoring

Once threats are identified, they are scored using DREAD (Damage, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected Users, Discoverability) or CVSS v3.1 to prioritize mitigation effort.

Example: GPS spoofing scores CVSS 9.1 (Critical) due to high damage potential and low attack complexity — making it top priority for countermeasures such as signal authentication and redundant navigation systems.

Step 5 — Implement Security Controls and Conduct Testing
  • Penetration testing to identify residual vulnerabilities before deployment
  • AI-based anomaly detection for real-time threat monitoring in OT environments
  • Security patching and update management aligned with IEC 62443-4-1 SDLC requirements

Ⅲ. Threat Modeling in Maritime & Port Technology R&D

🚢 Autonomous Ships

Threat: Hacking of AI-based navigation systems

Mitigation: AI model integrity verification + anomaly detection

🏗️ Smart Ports

Threat: Tampering with port logistics and cargo data

Mitigation: Network segmentation + AI-driven anomaly detection

📡 Marine IoT

Threat: Compromised IoT sensors injecting false data

Mitigation: IoT security certification + strong data encryption

⚓ Field Note

In practice, IACS UR E27 Type Approval documentation directly maps to threat modeling outputs. The Description of Security Capabilities, Test Procedures, and Management of Change Plan required by UR E27 are all strengthened — and easier to prepare — when a structured threat model has been run during the R&D phase.

Conclusion

Incorporating threat modeling in R&D is no longer optional — it is a strategic imperative. By identifying and addressing security risks at the design stage, maritime organizations can build resilient systems, satisfy regulatory requirements, and bring safer products to market faster.

For organizations developing technologies for autonomous ships, smart ports, and connected vessels — the time to integrate threat modeling is now, at the drawing board. 🚢🔐
